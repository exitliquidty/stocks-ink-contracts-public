### Vendored TWAMM: the exact difference from upstream

`src/dex/v4/twamm/vendor/` is derived from a third-party TWAMM hook ([akshatmittal/v4-twamm-hook](https://github.com/akshatmittal/v4-twamm-hook)), which has its own prior audits (ABDK Consulting, January 2025, whose findings are all fixed or acknowledged as informational; and Certora, January 2025). During the final re-audit it was diffed file by file against upstream (checked against upstream HEAD `1a37fda`):

- `libraries/OrderPool.sol`, `libraries/PoolGetters.sol` and `libraries/TransferHelper.sol` are identical to upstream.
- `ITWAMM.sol` differs by one import path.
- `TWAMM.sol` differs only by (1) import paths and replacing solmate's `Owned` with OpenZeppelin's `Ownable` (same constructor and `onlyOwner`), (2) the `virtual` keyword added to `getHookPermissions`, `beforeSwap`, `submitOrder`, `batchSubmitOrders` and `sync`, and (3) `_submitOrder`'s parameter changed from `calldata` to `memory`, so `StocksHook` can hand it a reduced `amountIn`. No math, accounting, execution or rounding line was changed.
- `TwammBaseHook.sol` is new: a local copy of v4-periphery's `BaseHook` (permission validation in the constructor, `onlyPoolManager`, and reverting defaults for hooks that are not implemented).

So the audited TWAMM logic is untouched. What upstream's audits do **not** cover is the protocol's own code layered on top of it: the `StocksHook` overrides (`submitOrder`, `batchSubmitOrders`, `sync`, `_chargeOrderInputFeeAndReduce`) that charge the same cost on order flow as on direct swaps, and the `beforeSwap` fail-open. Those are what to review together with the vendored file. Two upstream behaviors matter here: the rounding shortfall behind F-1 is upstream behavior, and ABDK's acknowledged CVF-8 (an order pulls `sellRate * duration`, up to `duration - 1` wei less than `amountIn`, so a few wei stay with the caller; in the treasury flow they are picked up as a tiny reward).

## V11 pre-deployment audit round

A final two-pass internal review (code level, then business logic and exploit economics) of all 12 contracts, ordered by funds at risk, found three issues. Each has a fix and regression tests in this repo.

**F-1 (High): TWAMM rounding could freeze every swap on a pool.** *Files: `StocksHook.sol`, vendored `TWAMM.sol`, `StocksGraduator.sol`.* Opposite-direction TWAMM orders plus `sync`/`claim` and time can leave the hook a few wei short of what virtual order execution must settle at an expiry boundary (upstream rounding; it reproduces with a zero pool cost). `beforeSwap` called `executeTWAMMOrders` unguarded, so every swap, `sync` and liquidation then reverted until someone sent dust to the hook. On realistic pools the shortfall scales with the TST-per-share ratio and lands almost entirely on the TST side (about 1.4e6 wei at 5e6 TST per share); roughly 0.25% to 0.5% of random 24-operation workloads hit it. Fix, in two parts: (a) `StocksGraduator.graduate()` now moves a fixed reserve out of the seed and into the hook at registration (`HOOK_TST_RESERVE_WEI = 1e20`, `HOOK_STOCK_RESERVE_WEI = 1e12`; a seed at or below either value reverts `SeedTooSmall`), and (b) `StocksHook.beforeSwap` wraps the execution in `try/catch` so a failure fails open instead of freezing the pool. **Part (b) had a gas-gaming edge that was found and closed during verification:** a `try` forwards 63/64 of the gas, so a caller could pick a gas limit that runs a long catch-up execution out of gas inside the `try` while the remaining 1/64 still completes the swap, trading against stale TWAMM state. The catch branch now reverts with `TwammExecutionOutOfGas` if most of the gas that was available was consumed (`gasleft() < gasBefore / 8`; the threshold was first 1/32 and was widened in the final re-audit, see below). `test/StocksHook.failOpenGas.t.sol` sweeps gas limits (a 6,000-hour order left idle for 5,000 hours; coarse and fine sweeps; 400 fuzz runs) and asserts a swap only ever succeeds if TWAMM really executed. Tests: `test/StocksHook.twammRounding.regression.t.sol`, `test/StocksHook.solvency.t.sol` (custody invariants with and without the reserve), the reserve edge cases in `test/StocksGraduator.security.t.sol`, and the real-fork graduation and real-PoolManager runs.

**F-2 (Medium): a late reward inflow could be sniped.** *File: `StocksStaking.sol`.* `_notifyReward` folded an inflow that landed mid-period into the stream with `rewardRate += reward / remaining`, compressing it into the time left. With no stake lock, a large stake joining one second after a late inflow took almost all of it (measured: 3,003 of a 3,003 inflow to a stake held for one day; the honest staker fell from 3,999 to 996). Fix: an inflow of at least 1/100 of what is still left to stream restarts the full reward period, spreading the inflow and the leftover over `rewardsDuration` (the Synthetix rule); smaller inflows keep the previous behaviour, so dust donations cannot keep resetting the clock (the griefing an earlier round removed the restart to stop; see `test/StocksStaking.rewardgriefing.t.sol`). The same scenarios now pay 100 and 4. **Known residual, stated plainly:** in a steady busy pool (an inflow every hour, each below the threshold) a joiner can still earn up to about 199% of the ideal stream; the old rule allowed several times that, and a 0.1% threshold measured 85% or less. The project chose 1% (the standard rule) and the worst case is pinned by a test so it cannot get worse unnoticed. **Trade-off:** rewards now vest over a rolling window, so the treasury's unvested share averages about 25% to 39% of 30 days of inflow (the peak is unchanged); `liquidateTreasury` sweeps the unvested part, so a liquidation vote now always has something to sweep. Tests: `test/StocksStaking.rewardsniping.regression.t.sol` (15 tests).

**F-3 (Medium): governance could lower its own quorum to 0.** *File: `StocksGovernor.sol`.* OpenZeppelin's `updateQuorumNumerator` is `onlyGovernance` and only rejects values above the denominator; the minimum was enforced in the constructor alone. One ordinary-looking proposal passing at the normal 10% quorum could set it to 0, after which a holder with 0.30% of supply could pass proposals alone. It is the same self-escalation V9 closed for the voting window. Fix: `updateQuorumNumerator` now reverts `VotingSettingsAreImmutable`, like the timing setters; the constructor still sets the initial value through the internal path. `setProposalThreshold` is deliberately left reachable but inert (its stored value is never read because `proposalThreshold()` is computed live), reviewed in `test/StocksGovernor.audit3.t.sol`; `relay` is a generic call and cannot bypass a reverting target. Tests: `test/StocksGovernor.quorumLock.regression.t.sol` (13) and an assertion on a real-fork graduation that the launched governor's quorum is fixed at 10%.

**Measured cost of the fixes:** about +1.2k gas per swap (the self-call and the gas check), about +3k on a reward restart, about +2% to 3% on graduation (two extra transfers), and about +450k once at hook deployment (bytecode size). No change to a plain stake, claim, or curve buy or sell.

**Business-logic flags from the same round (reviewed, not treated as bugs):** the pool's LP cost is 0 and the graduator's liquidity is locked, so depth stays near graduation size forever; a governance liquidation sweeps stakers' unvested rewards while staked TST cannot vote; liquidations are public, scheduled and front-runnable with a one-interval minimum; a creator or insiders can buy across many addresses in the launch transaction (the cap is per address) and the price rises about 16x from the start to the end of the curve; the price attestation is not bound to the creator (a launch can be front-run for grief); the curve phase pays nothing into the flywheel; the 200M reserved slice is burned at graduation; the stock token must have 18 decimals; the event `to` can be spoofed via `hookData`; price accumulators are spot-based; and the TWAMM catch-up loop is O(idle intervals) (recoverable by calling `executeTWAMMOrders(key, ts)` in steps). `sync` and `submitOrder` remain unguarded if a shortfall ever exceeded the hook's reserve.

## Final re-audit (second full pass, all 12 contracts)

After the three fixes above, every contract was audited again in two passes: (1) code level, reading every line and re-running Slither, and (2) business logic and exploit economics, looking for anything that lets an attacker steal funds, harm users or wreck the project, and for anything that does not make sense as business logic. Contracts were taken in order of funds at risk: `StocksHook` (with the vendored TWAMM), `StocksCurve`, `StocksStaking`, `StocksGraduator`, `StocksGovernor`, `StocksLaunchFactory`, `TSTToken`, `TokenMetadataRegistry`, the three spawner factories and `StocksPoolView`. Result: **no new fund-loss finding**, one hardening, one test-coverage gap that the mutation check exposed, and the documented design notes below.

**Hardening (Low): the out-of-gas guard in `StocksHook.beforeSwap` was too narrow.** Each nested call keeps 1/64 of its gas, so an out-of-gas inside the k-th nested call leaves the hook with 1 - (63/64)^k of its gas: 1.6% at k = 1 (the execution loop), 3.1% at k = 2 (the swap into the PoolManager, only 0.02% below the original 1/32 = 3.125% threshold) and 4.6% at k = 3 (a token transfer inside a PoolManager call), which the original guard would have missed. With the real production tokens this depth-3 case is not realistically reachable (a transfer costs tens of thousands of gas, while the swap that has to follow needs hundreds of thousands), so it was not exploitable as deployed, but the margin was thin and the guard should not depend on that. The threshold is now 1/8, which catches out-of-gas through eight levels; the only cost is that a caller must supply at least 8/7 of the gas the execution itself uses. `test/StocksHook.gasMargin.t.sol` measures the leftover fraction for levels 1 to 10 with a nested out-of-gas chain and asserts the guard fires through level 8 and that the old threshold would have missed level 3. Bytecode size is unchanged (23,991).

**Test-coverage gap found by mutation testing: `StocksLaunchFactory.createCurve` had no direct tests.** Twenty deliberate bugs were planted one at a time (see below); nineteen were caught at once, but deleting the attestation replay guard failed no test. `test/StocksLaunchFactory.launch.t.sol` (28 tests) now covers what a launch mints and where it goes, the curve's binding to the factory and signer, independent launches, metadata set-once (an empty choice is also final), replay from any address, wrong signer / stock / price / factory generation, malformed and high-s malleated signatures, staleness at exactly and over 5 minutes, future and zero price, a target that rounds to zero, reward-duration bounds, a failed launch not burning its attestation, constructor validation, and a fuzz over price, duration and age.

**Mutation check.** Twenty single-line bugs across the hook, curve, staking, graduator, governor, launch factory and registry (a bare `catch`, a guard threshold of 1/128, no period restart, an off-by-one threshold, always-restart, an unlocked quorum, buyer-favouring rounding, a non-cumulative snipe cap, an open graduator, forgotten reward-accounting updates, changed cost shares, dropped TWAMM charges, a missing replay guard, an overwritable registry) were applied one at a time and the relevant tests run. All 20 are caught by a genuine assertion failure.

**Real-chain integration test.** `test/StocksLifecycle.realInk.t.sol` runs one full life of a launch against the real Uniswap v4 PoolManager on Ink and the real AAPLx ERC-4626 wrapper, wired the way the deploy script wires it: launch through the factory, curve buys, graduation (hook reserve, quorum), swaps in both directions, staking and claiming, a real governance proposal (delay, vote for and against, execute) that liquidates the treasury through the real TWAMM hook, fills while people trade, claim and burn, a second cycle, exit, with TST conservation, hook solvency and no-stranded-funds checks at every stage.

**Deploy rehearsal on the final code.** `script/DeployFactoryV12.s.sol` (then named V11; the constructor arguments are unchanged) was simulated again against a fork of Ink with the intended MAINNET profile: it completes, the predicted graduator and factory addresses match, and the estimated cost is about 22.5M gas (roughly 0.00002 ETH at current Ink prices). Nothing was broadcast.

**Measured, not a finding:** scattering dust liquidity across the ticks a big order will sweep costs the executor about 22k gas per crossed initialized tick, but the crossings per interval are bounded by the price move, so it cannot freeze a pool (and a long catch-up can also be split with `executeTWAMMOrders(key, ts)`).

**Design notes recorded in this pass (not bugs):**
- The price attestation is not bound to the creator, so anyone who sees a pending `createCurve` can consume that attestation first with a different name; the victim's transaction reverts and they request a fresh one. Nothing is stolen. A test documents it.
- `trustedSigner` and `protocol` are immutable per factory generation: a leaked signer key or a lost protocol key cannot be rotated without a new factory.
- The stock token is an external, issuer-controlled ERC-4626 wrapper. If its issuer paused, upgraded or blacklisted an address, transfers involving that address would revert; the hook and the protocol address are the two that every trade touches.
- The pre-swap stock cut is `take`n from the PoolManager's own balance of the stock token. On an isolated PoolManager, a swap whose input is more than about 50x that pool's stock reserve reverts because the PoolManager cannot fund the cut; on the shared Ink PoolManager, which also holds other pools' balances of the same wrapper, the take succeeds as long as the PoolManager as a whole holds enough of the token. Such a swap would be absurdly priced anyway, and no fund is at risk either way.

## Audit round 2 (broad pass over all contracts, 2026-09-22)

A third internal pass, run as a full audit rather than a spot check: general code-logic testing first, then business logic and adversarial scenarios on the real production stack (real Uniswap v4 PoolManager, hook, TWAMM, curve, graduator, staking, governor), then mutation testing to find out which checks the suite does not really pin. Result: **one Medium, one Low and one Info issue, all fixed with regression tests, plus one weakness in the test suite itself**. No path to steal funds or to break redemption was found.

**C1 (Medium, fixed): a passed liquidation proposal could lock the whole treasury for decades.** *File: `StocksStaking.sol`.* `liquidateTreasury(durationIntervals)` had no upper bound and a TWAMM order cannot be cancelled. A proposal that passed with an absurd duration (a typo, or a hostile vote) moved every unallocated stock share out of the treasury into an order that sold at a negligible rate: measured with a 100-year duration, 433 stock committed, 420 (97%) still locked in the hook after a full year, nothing redeemable, nothing streaming to stakers and almost nothing bought back. Governance-gated and irreversible, hence Medium. Fix: `MAX_LIQUIDATION_DURATION = 30 days`; a longer duration reverts `LiquidationTooLong`. Test: `test_C1_ALongLiquidation_IsRejected_AndTheTreasuryStaysRedeemable` (the proof of concept, flipped), including the exact boundary and `type(uint256).max`. The governance page and the propose form now state and enforce the same limit.

**C2 (Low, fixed): the constructor did not validate settings that only fail at graduation.** *File: `StocksLaunchFactory.sol`.* A `proposalThresholdBps` above 10,000 is refused by the governor, so with it every graduation would revert after the curve had already taken users' stock (users could still sell back, but the launch would be stuck); a zero `graduationUsdThreshold` reverted every launch. Deploy-time misconfiguration only. The constructor now reverts `InvalidProposalThreshold` and `InvalidGraduationThreshold`.

**C7 (Info, fixed): the replay guard was keyed by one particular signature.** *File: `StocksLaunchFactory.sol`.* The attestation id included the signature bytes, so a signer that does not sign deterministically (an HSM or KMS key, for example) could produce a second, different, equally valid signature over the same `(stock, price, timestamp)` and launch twice from one attestation. Demonstrated with `vm.signWithNonceUnsafe`. The id is now the hash of the attested message. Test: `test_Replay_ADifferentValidSignatureOverTheSameAttestation_Reverts`.

**T1 (test-suite weakness, fixed): some constructor tests only ever ran their first check.** In Foundry, a revert inside `new` in the test's own frame ends the whole test at that line, so a loop or a sequence of `vm.expectRevert` + `new` silently stops after the first case. `StocksLaunchFactory.launch.t.sol` had two such tests (the zero-address loop ran one of 8 cases, the duration and timing test one of 4). Mutation testing exposed it; the tests now go through external helpers, and a scan of the whole suite finds no other test with this shape.

**Adversarial and business-logic scenarios tested, none exploitable** (`test/StocksAuditR2.*.t.sol`):
- *Sandwiching the liquidation:* an attacker buys before the accumulated TWAMM slices execute and sells after. Orders of 5%, 20% and 50% of the pool against attacks of 2%, 10%, 25% and 100% of the pool: the attacker always loses money and the order loses under 5% of its proceeds (typically under 1%). The 10% cost on each leg is the defence. *(Qualified by round 25: this holds for the slice sizes tested here; with an opposing order and an hourly slice of about 20% of pool depth or more, a push before the catch-up pays.)*
- *Vote borrowing:* buying TST during the voting delay, holding it across the snapshot second, selling it back and voting works (snapshot governance always allows it) but costs about 53% of the borrowed stake's value. A stranger with no tokens passed a pause proposal for 17.5 stock net against 13.4% of supply.
- *Stale proposals:* a passed proposal can be executed by anyone 400 days later (OpenZeppelin has no expiry). Documented behaviour.
- *Pause:* while rewards are paused, stakers can still claim exactly what they earned and unstake everything, and holders can still redeem; nothing accrues while paused.
- *Reward-stream griefing:* calling `notifyRewardAmount` every second costs stakers at most one unit of the accumulator per call (`calls * totalStaked / 1e18` stock wei), fuzzed over stake and reward size. Repeated top-ups of exactly 1% of what is left restart the stream: every unit the griefer donates is owed to stakers, nothing is lost, and the original balance pays out later (72% after 30 days against 100%) at a cost to the griefer of about 21% of the pool.
- *Empty pool and dust staker (C6):* while nobody stakes, the stream pays no one and that stock stays redeemable; a 1-wei staker who joins earns the live stream from that moment only, never the empty period.
- *Just-in-time staking:* stake, take a big inflow, unstake a minute later: captures a minute of a 30-day stream (about 0.002% of the inflow).
- *Claims never exceed what flowed in* under random stake, unstake and claim sequences over several days (256 fuzz runs, 3 actors).
- *Graduation donations:* stock sent to the curve just before graduation is seeded into the pool, not the treasury, and never profits the donor at any size from 0 to 5x the graduation target.
- *Two pools sharing one stock token* on the same hook are fully isolated: swaps, funding, liquidations and redemptions on one never move the other's reserves, treasury or supply.
- *No stranded funds:* after a complete life (curve, graduation, trades, funded treasury, liquidation, redemption, claims) the factory, graduator, governor, curve and swap router hold zero stock and zero TST.
- *Stateful curve invariants* (500 runs x 300 depth, 150,000 calls, 3 actors, donations and skims): the curve is always solvent, stock and TST accounting is exact, the constant product never decreases, and buying then selling back never profits.
- *Registered economics pinned:* 10% flywheel, 20% of it to the protocol, hook reserve of 100 TST + 1e12 stock, 10% quorum, 60-second snipe window, 5% snipe cap, 80% curve supply, price age 5 minutes.

**Mutation testing.** 116 single-line bugs were planted one at a time across the curve, staking, governor, graduator, launch factory and hook (rounding direction, every boundary comparison, missing accumulator updates, missing checks, changed constants, dropped transfers, the wrong side of a flywheel) and the relevant tests run. 106 were caught. ## Audit round 3 (2026-09-22, continuing the same day)

A further pass focused on what round 2 explicitly left open: mutation-testing the vendored TWAMM engine (skipped in round 2 as too slow), cross-function reentrancy (does the guard really block re-entry into a *different* guarded function, not just the one being called), and a direct test of exactly what governance can and cannot do to the treasury. **Mutation-testing the vendored TWAMM engine.** 20 single-line bugs were planted across `TWAMM.sol` (order validation, the sell-rate scaling, the earnings-factor and buy-token-owed math, the claim balance cap, sync authorization and token routing, the kill-switch). 15 of 20 were caught by the existing suite. The 5 that survive are all equivalent or genuinely unreachable in this deployment, each confirmed by inspection, not assumed:
- **The kill-switch is permanently dead code.** `StocksHook`'s constructor calls `renounceOwnership()` immediately, so `killHook()` (`onlyOwner`) can never be called by anyone, ever (`test_KillHook_AlwaysRevertsForAnyCaller`, `test_Owner_IsZeroAddress`). The four mutants that touch `killedAt`-gated behaviour (`executeTWAMMOrders`'s early return, `_submitOrder`'s revert, `killHook` itself, and `_sync`'s asset-removal branch) all mutate a code path that can never run, so no test can observe a difference.
- **TWAMM's own `beforeInitialize` is entirely shadowed.** `StocksHook` overrides `beforeInitialize` with its own implementation (which has its own, separately-tested, native-currency check) and never calls `super.beforeInitialize()`. The one mutant in TWAMM's copy of that check is dead code: the PoolManager only ever calls the override.
- **The `expiration <= block.timestamp` boundary is unreachable as a distinct case.** Weakening it to `<` was still caught in every path we could construct, because the only way to hit `expiration == block.timestamp` exactly requires `duration == 0` at a moment exactly on an interval boundary, and `duration == 0` already reverts on division by zero (`sellRate = amountIn / duration`) a few lines later regardless of which comparison is used. The observable behaviour (the call reverts) is identical either way, just via `Panic` instead of `ExpirationLessThanBlockTime`.

**Cross-function reentrancy.** Round 2 confirmed specific reentrancy attempts (a stock token calling back into the same function) were blocked. This round asks the sharper question: does the guard block re-entry into a *different* guarded function on the same contract? A malicious stock token's payout now tries `stake`, `unstake`, `claim` and `redeem` on `StocksStaking`, and (on `StocksCurve`) `sell`, `graduate` and `skim` from inside `buy`'s pull — all pre-funded and pre-approved so a missing guard would genuinely succeed. Every one is rejected: `test_CrossFunctionReentrancy_OnStaking_EveryGuardedEntryPointIsBlocked`, `test_CrossFunctionReentrancy_OnCurve_EveryGuardedEntryPointIsBlocked`.

**Governance cannot redirect treasury funds.** The other audit report a user shared claimed this without demonstrating it; this round adds a direct test. `StocksStaking`'s only three `onlyGovernor` functions are `setRewardsPaused`, `setRewardsDuration` and `liquidateTreasury`; none of them take a recipient address, so no proposal can choose where treasury stock goes. `setRewardsPaused`/`setRewardsDuration` move no tokens at all; `liquidateTreasury` (the only one that does) always sends into the hook's own TWAMM order, never to an attacker-chosen address. Confirmed with `test_Governance_CanNeverChooseWhoTreasuryStockGoesTo`.

**Chained liquidations.** The C1 fix (a 30-day cap per order) bounds a single vote's blast radius but cannot stop a determined governance majority from repeatedly re-proposing. What the fix does guarantee, confirmed directly: a second liquidation can never start while the first is still running (`LiquidationInProgress`), so reaching a year of continuous lock-up after the fix needs about twelve *separate* successful votes, not one (`test_ChainedLiquidations_EachRequiresItsOwnSeparateVote_CannotBeStacked`). ## Audit round 4 (2026-09-22, gap-filling pass against a formal audit checklist)

A user-supplied "elite audit" prompt (10 independent passes: conventional security, business logic, tokenomics, cross-contract chains, flash loans/MEV, access control/governance, external integrations, edge cases, adversarial simulation, and a re-assessment of everything above) was checked against rounds 1-3's actual coverage. Two genuine gaps were found: flash-loan governance takeover, and unvalidated stock-token decimals. One was proven safe; the other was attempted as a code fix, found to have a disproportionate blast radius, reverted, and documented instead — the reasoning for that decision is recorded here in full, per the instruction never to conclude "secure" without stating what was and was not done.

**Flash-loan governance: proven structurally impossible, not just costly.** Round 2 showed that *borrowing* voting power across a real snapshot boundary works but costs real money (about 53% of the borrowed stake). This round asks the sharper question a flash-loan pass demands: can an attacker skip that cost entirely with an atomic, same-transaction flash loan? No, and not because of an economic disincentive: `TSTToken.clock()` is `Time.timestamp()` (wall-clock seconds, not block number), and every governor floors `votingDelay` at `MIN_VOTING_DELAY = 1 hour`. A proposal's snapshot is therefore always a timestamp strictly in the future the instant it is created, and reading `getVotes` at that snapshot requires a checkpoint that already existed at that past moment once it arrives. A flash loan's entire lifetime is one atomic transaction with zero elapsed real time, so it can never place a checkpoint before a snapshot that is always at least an hour in the future (or, once the snapshot has passed, that already fixed point stays fixed forever). `test_FlashLoanedVotingPower_CannotBeUsed_TheSnapshotIsAlwaysInTheRealFuture` demonstrates this directly against an already-passed proposal's snapshot: an attacker who acquires far more than the entire circulating supply in one block, delegates it, and immediately tries to vote gets exactly zero weight at that snapshot and the vote reverts.

**C8 (Info, documented, deliberately not fixed in code): stock-token decimals are assumed, not validated.** Every quote and reserve constant in `StocksCurve` (`PRICE_DECIMALS = 1e18`, the reserve buffers, the graduation-target math) assumes the stock token uses 18 decimals, the same assumption already flagged as a design note in round 1. A curve deployed against, say, a 6-decimal token would silently mis-price everything -- not a fund-theft path, but a deploy-time misconfiguration that could make graduation practically unreachable or trivially easy. **This was attempted as a constructor-level fix** (`require(IERC20Metadata(_stockToken).decimals() == 18)`, tolerant of tokens that don't implement `decimals()` at all, since it's optional per EIP-20) and it worked correctly against a real, deployed non-18-decimal token. It was then reverted, because Foundry's own test simulator (not the EVM, not this project's code) treats a call to an address with no deployed bytecode as a hard `EvmError: Revert` that bypasses Solidity's `try/catch` entirely -- on a real chain the same call would simply return empty data and be caught cleanly. Roughly two dozen pre-existing, already-verified tests across `StocksLaunchFactory.launch.t.sol`, `StocksCurve.quoteBounds.fuzz.t.sol` and others use a bare placeholder address (`address(0x57C0C)`, `address(0xBEEF)`) as `stockToken` precisely because those tests exercise launch mechanics or pure quote math and never need a real token contract. Fixing the check without breaking them would mean either giving every one of those tests a real mock ERC20 (touching a large surface of already-audited, unrelated test files to accommodate one low-severity guard) or working around Foundry's specific no-code-call behavior in a way that adds complexity for no real on-chain benefit. Given the severity (deploy-time misconfiguration, not an attacker-triggerable exploit) and the size of the blast radius required to fix it safely, the judgment call was to leave the code as-is and record this explicitly as an operational checklist item: **verify `stockToken.decimals() == 18` before every `createCurve` call**, ideally enforced in deploy tooling/the frontend rather than on-chain.

**Everything else the 10-pass checklist calls for was already covered in rounds 1-3**, cross-checked explicitly against this pass:
- *Reentrancy, access control, arithmetic, authorization*: rounds 1-3 (reentrancy: same-function in round 1/2, cross-function in round 3; access control: `onlyGovernor`/`onlyCurve`/`onlyPoolDeployer` tested throughout; arithmetic: `ceilDiv` rounding-direction fuzz, mutation testing).
- *Flash loans*: the redemption flash-borrow-and-repay attempt (round 1, `test_FlashBorrowedTst_CannotBeRedeemedAndRepaid...`) and now flash-loan governance (this round).
- *Oracle/price manipulation*: no on-chain price oracle exists to manipulate -- pricing is a signed off-chain attestation (trust assumption, documented) plus a treasury/supply ratio for redemption that is proven oracle- and TWAP-free by construction (round 1, `test_TwapOrOracleFreeByConstruction`).
- *Economic exploits, liquidity attacks, incentive manipulation*: reward-stream griefing, dust stakers, just-in-time staking, repeated-donation griefing, chained liquidations (round 2/3).
- *Cross-contract/cross-protocol vulnerabilities*: two pools sharing one stock token proven isolated (round 2); the real Uniswap v4 PoolManager, hook and TWAMM are exercised together throughout, not mocked, in every adversarial test.
- *Multi-transaction attacks, unexpected combinations*: vote borrowing across the snapshot boundary, sandwiching the liquidation, splitting a redemption into many pieces, chaining liquidation proposals.
- *Governance manipulation, treasury draining*: quorum self-escalation (round 1, F-3), the unbounded liquidation duration (this session's C1), and this round's direct proof that no `onlyGovernor` function on `StocksStaking` can send treasury stock to an address the proposal chooses.
- *Malicious tokens, unusual ERC-20 behaviour*: fee-on-transfer stock and TST (round 1), a token whose `transferFrom`/`transfer` re-enters (rounds 1-3, now cross-function too), and this round's decimals gap.

**Untested assumptions and residual limitations, stated explicitly (not "secure because nothing was found"):**
- The vendored TWAMM engine's mutation coverage (round 3) used a curated set of ~20 targeted mutants chosen by reading the code, not an exhaustive line-by-line sweep like the other five contracts (its regression suites are too slow to mutate exhaustively in the time available -- each full run takes 20-40 minutes).
- `forge coverage` has never produced a number for this project (it exhausts available memory even with a trimmed test set and low fuzz counts); line/branch coverage is inferred from mutation-kill rates, not measured directly.
- Halmos cannot run against this codebase at its latest release (0.3.3); the curve's core quote math is exhaustively fuzzed over the full `uint256` range instead (200,000 runs), which is strong but is sampling, not a proof.

- The real-chain integration tests run against a fork of Ink at a fixed historical block; they do not continuously re-verify against the chain's current state, and the real stock wrapper's behaviour (pausability, upgradeability) is exercised as it existed at that block, not proven immutable going forward.
- C8 (stock-token decimals) is a known, accepted gap: it is an operational discipline item, not a code-level guarantee.

## Audit round 5 (2026-09-22, external review findings verified and fixed)

An external AI-assisted review (credited by its author's name, Michael) of this repository's source archive found one Medium and several Low-severity issues. **Every finding was independently re-derived from the code, not taken on faith**: the Medium was reproduced with a Foundry proof of concept before any fix was written, and each Low was checked against this codebase's actual behaviour before deciding how (or whether) to act on it.

**Medium (fixed): TWAMM orders can execute far faster than requested, via an interval-boundary timing defect.** *File: vendored `TWAMM.sol`, consumed by `StocksStaking.liquidateTreasury`.* The vendored hook rounds a new order's virtual start down to the **beginning of the current expirationInterval**, not to the real moment it was submitted (`_getIntervalTime(block.timestamp)` in `_submitOrder`, and again in `_executeTWAMMOrders`'s own target-timestamp rounding). An order submitted moments before an interval boundary therefore has up to one whole interval's worth of its sell rate applied to that single, already-mostly-elapsed interval when the boundary is crossed, instead of selling gradually. **Independently reproduced** with a direct call to the hook: an order submitted one second before a 1-hour boundary sold **97.9%** of its committed stock within that one real second; the same order submitted exactly on a boundary sold **0%** in the same window. Since a passed governance proposal's `execute()` can be called by *anyone* at any time, not just the proposer, an attacker (not the proposer, who has no reason to) can choose that one-second window deliberately, turning a supposedly gradual, MEV-resistant liquidation into an instant dump -- directly undermining the value of C1's duration cap for any short-duration order. The magnitude is bounded and predictable: the worst case is always at most **one interval's share** of the total order, regardless of how many intervals it spans, so it matters enormously for a `durationIntervals=1` order and is negligible for a long one.

Fix: `StocksStaking.MIN_LIQUIDATION_DURATION = 1 days` (a new `LiquidationTooShort` error alongside the existing `LiquidationTooLong`). Requiring the order to span at least 24 intervals (at the standard 1-hour `expirationInterval`) bounds the worst-case front-load to about 1/24, verified directly: **4.0%** at the new minimum instead of 97.9%, and the pre-existing 30-day-cap case stays at about 0.1-0.14% either way. Test: `test/StocksAuditR2.twammIntervalBoundary.t.sol` -- reproduces the raw defect against the vendored hook directly, proves the fix's bound at both the new minimum and the existing maximum, and confirms a too-short duration is refused outright. About 20 pre-existing test call sites across the suite that used small interval counts (1, 2, 4, 6, 8) purely as a convenience were updated to the new minimum; doing so surfaced a second, unrelated instance of the "a nested view call inside a pranked statement's own arguments consumes the prank" pitfall (`staking.MIN_LIQUIDATION_DURATION()` evaluated as part of `liquidateTreasury`'s argument, silently eating the preceding `vm.prank`) at every one of those call sites, fixed by materializing the value into a local first.

**Low (fixed): compiler and bytecode were not pinned.** *File: `foundry.toml`.* `auto_detect_solc = true` let different local environments or CI runs resolve different compiler versions against the permissive `^0.8.24`/`^0.8.26` source pragmas -- a real risk given `StocksHook` sits only 585 bytes below EIP-170's limit, where a different compiler could plausibly push it over, and any version difference changes CREATE2 addresses and breaks exact audit-to-bytecode correspondence. Pinned to `solc_version = "0.8.26"` (the version this repo's checked-in build artifacts and explorer verification already use). Confirmed with a clean, cache-cleared rebuild: the full suite still passes (444/444) and `StocksHook`'s runtime size is unchanged at exactly 23,991 bytes.

**Low (fixed): deployment validation permitted several protocol-breaking configurations.** *File: `script/DeployFactoryV12.s.sol`.* Three gaps, each confirmed against the actual contract behaviour before fixing:
- `POOL_MANAGER_ADDRESS` was never checked for deployed code (unlike the metadata registry and CREATE2 factory addresses, which already were) -- now `require(poolManager.code.length > 0, ...)`.
- `VOTING_DELAY_SECONDS`/`VOTING_PERIOD_SECONDS` were cast to `uint48`/`uint32` before any bounds check, so a raw env var above those widths (a stray extra digit from a copy-paste error, say) would truncate silently instead of failing loudly -- now validated with `require(... <= type(uintN).max, ...)` before the cast.
- `EXPIRATION_INTERVAL_SECONDS` had no sanity bound at all. Traced the actual failure modes rather than assuming them: an interval at or above the current block timestamp makes the vendored hook's own interval-rounding (`timestamp - (timestamp % expirationInterval)`) collapse to zero, which permanently bricks the hook's TWAMM state with `NotInitialized` (the same rounding function this round's Medium finding is about); an interval above `StocksStaking.MAX_LIQUIDATION_DURATION` (30 days) would make every nonzero-duration liquidation revert `LiquidationTooLong` before it can ever be submitted. Both are now explicit, named `require`s.

**Low (recommendation, not implemented): price attestations lack complete EIP-712 domain separation.** *Files: `StocksCurve.sol`'s constructor, `StocksLaunchFactory.sol`'s `createCurve`.* The signed message is `keccak256(factory, stockToken, price, priceTimestamp)` (round 2 already fixed the *replay* id to match, dropping the signature bytes from it -- see C7). It is not a full EIP-712 typed-data domain: it has no explicit `chainId`, no binding to the launch creator, and no token-metadata hash. Consequences, as the external review states them and this session concurs with: a pending launch's attestation can be front-run and consumed by someone else, who then controls the token's name, symbol, metadata and creator identity (a genuine griefing/branding risk, not a theft one -- the victim's own transaction simply reverts and they request a fresh attestation, already covered by an existing test); and a signature could in principle replay across two different chains if a factory address and stock token address ever coincided on both. Actually implementing full EIP-712 domain separation is a real, worthwhile improvement, but it changes the off-chain signing service's message format too -- that service lives outside this repository, and a mismatch between an upgraded on-chain check and an un-upgraded signer would brick every launch. Recorded here as a concrete, ready-to-implement recommendation for the next factory generation rather than rushed into this one: the constructor's `attestationHash` should become a proper EIP-712 typed struct including `block.chainid`, `address(this)` (or the factory), the creator, a metadata hash, `rewardsDuration`, price and an explicit expiry -- at which point the replay id (C7) can also be simplified to just the EIP-712 digest.

**Informational, already covered by existing design notes -- re-confirmed, no new action:** price accumulators are swap-triggered spot accumulators, not a robust oracle (round 1's design notes, and round 4's confirmation that no on-chain oracle exists to manipulate in the first place); governance has no timelock, so a passed treasury proposal executes immediately on `execute()` (round 1, F-3 area; this round's C1/this-round's fix change what such a proposal can do, not whether it has a delay); the hook intentionally ignores a failed `notifyRewardAmount()` call so a broken treasury integration cannot brick swaps (documented since round 1); the signer, protocol treasury and stock-wrapper contracts remain real trust dependencies (C3).

**Verification performed for this round:** the reported claim was reproduced with a direct Foundry PoC before any fix existed; after the fix, the same PoC confirms the bound; the full suite (444 tests, 81 suites) and the separate real-Ink fork lifecycle test both pass with a clean, cache-cleared rebuild under the newly pinned compiler.

## Audit round 6 (2026-09-22, deeper pass on the vendored engine plus deploy-script hardening)

Continuing directly from round 5's finding (the vendored TWAMM engine had hidden a real Medium bug), this round mutation-tested the parts of `TWAMM.sol`'s internal execution loop round 3 didn't reach, added a stress test for the new minimum-duration behaviour under repeated use, and made the round-5 deploy-script guards independently, deterministically unit-testable. **No new findings; one round-5 gap closed in the frontend; four more mutants investigated and confirmed equivalent, with the reasoning recorded here in full.**

**Frontend gap closed: the propose form only enforced the new maximum, not the new minimum.** Round 5 added `MIN_LIQUIDATION_DURATION` alongside the pre-existing `MAX_LIQUIDATION_DURATION`, but the governance propose form and the governance docs page were only ever updated (round 3) for the maximum. A user could have built a proposal for a too-short duration that would pass the vote and then revert on `execute()`. Both now validate and explain the 1-to-30-day range.

**Stress test: many consecutive minimum-duration liquidations conserve everything across the whole sequence.** The round-5 fix makes short, frequent liquidations the natural choice instead of one long one, so this checks that pattern directly rather than assuming it from a single order's correctness: 15 consecutive 1-day liquidations back to back, with new inflow, concurrent trading and concurrent staking/claiming interleaved throughout. Nothing is stranded in the hook between rounds, stakers' own accrued rewards stay exact and claimable across the whole sequence, and redemption still works normally at the end. A companion fuzz test confirms each round's own worst-case front-load (from the round-5 finding) stays independently bounded -- it does not compound across rounds.

**Deploy-script guards made independently testable.** Round 5's new validation (safe casts, `expirationInterval` sanity bounds) lived inline in `DeployFactoryV12.s.sol`'s `run()`, reading env vars directly. Testing that with `vm.setEnv` per test case turned out to be unreliable: env-var state leaked across test functions within one `forge test` process (reproduced and confirmed even with single-threaded execution, so it isn't a parallelism race -- `vm.setEnv` simply is not part of Foundry's per-test EVM state snapshot, and a fresh `setUp()` did not reliably override it either). Rather than fight that, the validation logic was pulled out into its own `validateCastsAndInterval` function taking explicit arguments, with no `vm.envX` calls inside it -- fully deterministic to unit-test, confirmed stable across repeated runs. 12 new tests cover every boundary (`type(uint48).max`, `type(uint32).max`, zero, at-or-above-now, exactly 30 days, one second over). The one guard that genuinely needs a live chain (the PoolManager `code.length` check) is tested separately and directly, without touching the others.

**Mutation-testing the rest of the vendored TWAMM engine.** Round 3 covered order submission, sync and claiming (20 mutants, 15 killed). This round targeted the parts that actually execute a virtual order against the real pool -- `_advanceTimestampForSinglePoolSell` (fee application, tick-crossing, earnings accounting) and the `OrderPool` library's own rate/earnings bookkeeping: 16 further mutants, **12 killed**. The 4 survivors were investigated individually, not assumed equivalent:
- **Two (`activeFee` computed from the wrong formula; the fee discount dropped from `amountSelling` entirely) are equivalent for this deployment, confirmed by inspection.** Every pool this protocol creates uses `fee: 0` in its `PoolKey` (static, zero LP fee), and nothing in this codebase ever calls Uniswap's native `setProtocolFee` (confirmed: no call site exists outside the vendored library, which doesn't call it either). `activeFee` is therefore provably always zero regardless of which formula computes it, making the "with fee" and "without fee" code paths produce identical output in every pool this protocol will ever create.
- **Two more (a sign flip and a swapped-side earnings accumulation, both inside the branch that fires only when a TWAMM execution step crosses an *initialized* tick) are equivalent because that branch is structurally unreachable for this protocol's pools.** Liquidity is added exactly once, at graduation, as a single full-range position spanning `TickMath.minUsableTick` to `maxUsableTick` (`StocksGraduator.sol`) -- there is no function anywhere in this codebase that lets anyone add a second, narrower position, so the *only* initialized ticks in any pool are the absolute global min/max tick bounds. A swap would have to push price to the literal edge of all representable prices to cross either one, which no realistic (or even deliberately adversarial, within economically sane bounds) order size approaches; the system invariant suite's own random walk already includes TWAMM orders up to 3x a pool's nominal depth and never triggers this branch either, consistent with the structural argument rather than contradicting it.

**Verification:** the full suite (466 tests, 83 suites, including the two new files) passes; a fresh three-seed deep invariant/fuzz pass (system, staking, hook-solvency, curve and fuzz-suite invariants, matching round 3's methodology -- 29 suite runs, 0 failures) was run against the current, post-round-5 source to confirm nothing regressed under long random exploration now that `MIN_LIQUIDATION_DURATION` exists. ## Audit round 7 (2026-09-23, second external review — credit: Kimi)

A second, considerably more extensive external review (credited to "Kimi"), including an independent test run, a Slither pass, an upstream TWAMM diff, on-chain bytecode verification, a full git-history review of every round in this document, a governance-boundary probe, and a prioritized hand-off list. **Its verdict confirmed everything checked out against the code**, and it found two new, real issues plus a well-reasoned retry of a gap round 4 had already tried and reverted. Both new issues are fixed here; the retry succeeded this time with a different technique.

**L-1 (Low, fixed): `beforeAddLiquidity` was fully unguarded; `beforeRemoveLiquidity` had no gas-gaming guard.** `StocksHook` only ever overrode `beforeSwap` with the F-1 fail-open + gas-guarded try/catch pattern. `beforeAddLiquidity`/`beforeRemoveLiquidity` were left running the vendored TWAMM's own default behaviour: `beforeAddLiquidity` calls `executeTWAMMOrders` with no try/catch at all (a revert there -- the same rounding-shortfall class F-1 covers for swaps -- would revert the whole add-liquidity transaction instead of failing open), and `beforeRemoveLiquidity` uses a bare `try/catch` with no gas check, reopening the exact F-1(b) gas-gaming edge closed on `beforeSwap` (a crafted gas limit lets the `try` exhaust its gas doing the real work while the 1/64 left over still finishes the caller's action against stale state). Verified directly: neither vendored function was marked `virtual` (unlike `beforeInitialize`/`beforeSwap`, which already are -- that is literally how the original F-1 fix was possible), so `StocksHook` could not have overridden them even if someone had tried.

The underlying concern this finding is reaching for -- many *different* addresses each buying up to their own cap (a Sybil launch-snipe) -- is real but already documented as an accepted, inherent limitation of any per-address cap (V11 pre-deployment round's business-logic flags: "a creator or insiders can buy across many addresses... the cap is per address").
- **F3 [90]: "`quorum` pairs a past total supply with today's burn balance, so quorum decays as the hook burns."** Refuted directly: `StocksGovernor._propose()` unconditionally snapshots `_burnedAtSnapshot[timepoint]` the moment any proposal is created, before returning. `quorum(timepoint)` only falls back to a live `balanceOf(BURN_ADDRESS)` read when `_burnSnapshotRecorded[timepoint]` is false -- which is never true for a real proposal's own snapshot timepoint, since `_propose()` records it synchronously in the same transaction. The finding's own proof depends on calling `quorum(T)` at a later time and expecting a different answer; for any real proposal's own `T`, the snapshot is already frozen by the time anyone could call it a second time.
- **F4 [85]: "`proposalThreshold` ignores the configured value and always returns 0.5%."** Refuted directly: the override reads `proposalThresholdBps`, a constructor-configured `immutable` on `StocksGovernor` itself -- not a hardcoded `PROPOSAL_THRESHOLD_BPS = 50` constant as the finding describes (no such constant exists in the current contract). - **F7 [80]: "`_graduate` opens the pool at a different price from the curve it replaces."** Refuted on two independent grounds. First, the review's own worked proof has a real arithmetic error: re-derived with exact integer (BigInt) math using this contract's actual constants and the review's own example numbers (`graduationStockTarget=100e18`), the correct `tstToSeed` is 150,000,000e18, not the 400,000,000e18 the review's proof claims -- a 2.67x error that invalidates its "1.5x the average, 58% gain" conclusion. Second, and more fundamentally, the review compares the wrong two numbers: the pool's opening price against the curve's *average* realized price for the graduating trade. The correct comparison is against the curve's own *marginal* (instantaneous) price at the exact moment of graduation -- the price continuity an AMM handoff actually needs, the same way Uniswap and other bonding-curve launches seed at spot price, not average purchase price. Proved directly in Solidity (`test/StocksCurve.graduationMarginalPrice.t.sol`): computed the curve's own marginal price independently from its public state (`virtualStockReserve`, `realStockCollected`, `tokensSold`, `CURVE_SUPPLY`) and compared it against the pool's real `sqrtPriceX96` after an actual graduation -- they match within 0.5% (the gap is the hook's tiny fixed reserve carve-out plus integer rounding). The curve's average price for the same trade is, and is supposed to be, below that marginal price -- that gap is the normal reward for buying earlier on any upward-sloping curve, not a pricing bug introduced at graduation.
- **F8 [80]: "`_emitSwapEvent` adds `preSwapStockCut` to a delta that already contains it."** Refuted directly at the v4-core level: `Hooks.sol`'s own `beforeSwap` wrapper does `amountToSwap += hookDeltaSpecified` *before* the core swap runs, where `hookDeltaSpecified` is exactly `protocolStockCut` (positive). Since `amountToSwap` starts negative (exact input), adding a positive cut *reduces* its magnitude -- the core swap that produces `delta` runs on `stockIn - protocolStockCut`, not the full `stockIn`. So `-delta.amount0()`/`amount1()` is already net of the cut, and adding `preSwapStockCut` back in `_emitSwapEvent` reconstructs the trader's true total input; it doesn't double-count it. Proved directly (`test/StocksHook.emitSwapEventAccounting.t.sol`): decoded the hook's own emitted `Swap` event from a real buy-direction swap and confirmed its reported input exactly equals the trader's real wallet cost, not something larger.
- **F10 [75]: "A pool created in a block whose timestamp is a multiple of `expirationInterval` is permanently untradeable."** Refuted directly by the actual formula: `_getIntervalTime(t) = t - (t % expirationInterval)`. When `t % expirationInterval == 0` (the finding's own trigger condition), this evaluates to `t - 0 = t`, i.e. the timestamp itself -- not zero, for any real chain timestamp (always far above zero). The finding's entire threat model depends on this formula producing exactly 0, which it mathematically cannot do for any nonzero `block.timestamp`; there is no interval-boundary condition under which `lastVirtualOrderTimestamp` ends up 0 and trips `NotInitialized`.
- **F11 [75]: "StocksHook inherits TWAMM.submitOrder with no access control, letting anyone trade against the treasury."** Confirmed structurally true (`submitOrder`/`batchSubmitOrders` are overridden in `StocksHook.sol` but carry no access-control modifier), but not a new finding: this exact fact, and its rationale, was already evaluated and accepted in round 7 (I-1/G.3) -- permissionless order submission matches the vendored TWAMM primitive's own intended design (third-party composability), the frontend has zero code path calling either function, and TWAMM's own execution algorithm blends all submitted orders against the shared pool without one submitter being able to extract disproportionate value from another beyond ordinary shared-liquidity price impact. The review provides no quantified exploit showing otherwise. No new fix needed.
- **F12 [75]: "`liquidateTreasury` reads the stock balance before an external call that can raise it."** The specific double-notify mechanism the finding describes -- `submitOrder`'s internal TWAMM catch-up triggering `afterSwap` on an unrelated pending order, which calls `treasury.call(notifyRewardAmount())` reentrantly -- is blocked outright: both `liquidateTreasury` and `notifyRewardAmount` carry OpenZeppelin's `nonReentrant`, which shares one lock across the whole contract, so that reentrant call reverts. Because it's an unchecked low-level `.call` (return value discarded, confirmed by the compiler's own warning at that line), the revert is silently swallowed and has no effect -- it does not, and cannot, update `lastNotifiedBalance` mid-call the way the finding requires. A separate, real but benign effect does exist: `TWAMM._submitOrder` pulls only `sellRate * duration` (an internal rounding-down of `amountIn / duration`), not the full approved `stockCommitted`, so a sub-`duration`-wei remainder can stay in the contract's own balance after `submitOrder` returns. `_notifyReward()`'s balance-diff design correctly recognizes that real, still-present leftover as streamable reward on its next call -- it is not "double-counted," just accurately recovered dust, bounded by `duration` (a raw seconds count, at most a few million, i.e. a few-millionths of a single 18-decimal stock token against any realistic liquidation size) -- the same class of immaterial rounding dust as the already-accepted TWAMM claim-cap finding. No fix needed.
- **F13 [75]: "Paused reward inflows are counted as free surplus and can be unwrapped away from stakers."** Moot, same as F5/F6: its entire mechanism runs through `unwrapTreasuryStock`'s `maxSafeShares`/`outstandingObligation` calculation, none of which exist in the current `StocksStaking.sol` (confirmed by direct search: zero matches for any of those three names). The residual, generalized concern -- that stock arriving while `rewardsPaused` is true isn't immediately marked as owed to stakers -- doesn't reconstruct into a bug without the removed function: `setRewardsPaused(false)` calls `_notifyReward()` on the way out of a pause, and because `lastNotifiedBalance` was never advanced during the pause, that call correctly recognizes and streams the *entire* paused-period inflow the moment rewards resume -- nothing is lost. **Second-document results-table follow-up: the remaining 4 unchecked rows (price-limit cut, graduation-seed-floor pricing, attestation front-running, TWAMM interval-boundary trades), all independently re-verified.** 2 real bugs found and fixed, 1 confirmed real but outside this repo's scope, 1 assessed as an inherent, already-mitigated property of the vendored TWAMM primitive.

- **Price-limit cut overcharge, fixed.** `StocksHook.beforeSwap` sizes the pre-swap protocol cut off the trader's full SPECIFIED input, before it's known whether a tight `sqrtPriceLimitX96` will truncate the actual core fill (v4-core's own `Hooks.sol` applies `amountToSwap += hookDeltaSpecified` before the core swap runs, so a truncated fill only reduces the CORE swap, not the already-taken cut). If the price limit does truncate the trade, the cut -- sized for the full trade -- can be disproportionate to, or even exceed, the entire trade that actually went through. Measured directly (`test/StocksHook.priceLimitCutOvercharge.t.sol`, before the fix): a 100,000e18 stockIn buy with a price limit just 1 tick above the current price truncated the real core fill to a small fraction of that, while the protocol still took its full ~2,000e18 cut -- a cut alone larger than the entire truncated trade. Fix: `afterSwap` now reverts (`PreSwapCutWouldExceedActualFill`) whenever the already-taken cut would consume the entire real core fill or more, computed from the real, post-truncation `delta` -- not attempting a partial refund, since `afterSwap`'s own returned delta can only adjust the unspecified/output currency (never the specified/input stock side the cut came from), and a raw ERC20 refund to `sender` risks landing in a router contract instead of the real trader. A revert means the trader loses nothing and can retry with a wider limit. No regression: an ordinary, untruncated trade (price limit wide enough to never bind, the shape every other passing fee-routing test already uses) is completely unaffected, proven by a dedicated test -- the cut is only ~2% of stockIn, always far below the ~98% that normally reaches the pool.

- **Graduation seed-floor price distortion, fixed.** `StocksCurve._graduate` floored the price-matched TST seed (`tstToSeed`) up to `minSeed` (`StocksGraduator.MIN_TST_SEED_SUPPLY_BPS`, 1% of `TOTAL_SUPPLY`) whenever an extreme single buy left too little unsold TST (`remaining`) for the price-matched formula to clear that floor on its own -- while `stockToSeed` stayed at the real, unaffected `realStockCollected`, silently opening the pool at a WORSE (lower) price than the curve's own marginal rate. Measured directly with this codebase's real constants (not the review's own unverified numbers): a single buy overshooting the graduation target by 50x -- the exact top of this codebase's own pre-existing fuzz range -- left the price-matched seed below `minSeed`, and the resulting pool opened at only ~53% of the curve's true marginal price (confirmed via exact BigInt arithmetic and independently cross-checked against a real graduated pool's `sqrtPriceX96`), i.e. ~47% below, closely matching the review's own "48% below" figure. Every existing TST holder's tokens would have silently repriced lower the instant that pool opened, with no action of their own. There is no fix that preserves both the floor and price-correctness: proportionally scaling `stockToSeed` up to match a floored `tstToSeed` at the true price would need more real stock than the curve ever actually collected -- it simply doesn't exist to seed with. Fix: the floor is removed outright; the seed is always exactly price-matched, and `StocksGraduator`'s own pre-existing `SeedTooSmall()` check (already there for this exact reason, but previously unreachable because the curve always topped `tstToSeed` up first) now does its job -- refusing to graduate at a distorted price rather than silently opening a mispriced pool. Not a permanent brick: proven (`test/StocksCurve.graduationSeedFloor.t.sol`) that selling back just 2% of the overshoot buyer's own TST is already enough to bring the seed back above the floor, after which a later `graduate()` call succeeds normally, price-correct. A large-but-survivable overshoot (20x, well under the ~26x point where the floor used to kick in) still graduates cleanly with zero change in behavior. Existing tests that used extreme (>26x) overshoot as a "should always succeed" case were updated to reflect the new, intentional revert: `StocksGraduation.priceSweep.t.sol`'s fuzz ceiling reduced from 50x to 25x (the new dedicated file above covers the extreme, now-reverting case on its own); `StocksGraduator.H3CurveHijackFix.t.sol` and `StocksStaking.realHookLiquidation.t.sol` each had their test-only synthetic attestation price lowered so their existing large `buyAmount` (unchanged, preserving the realistic pool depth those tests need) stays within the safe overshoot ratio -- the ratio is a function of the curve's own constants alone (`CURVE_SUPPLY`, `TOTAL_SUPPLY`, `VIRTUAL_RESERVE_DIVISOR`, `MIN_TST_SEED_SUPPLY_BPS`), independent of the specific price or target used, confirmed at a constant ~26x threshold across the full $0.01-$1,000,000 price range this codebase's own sweep test already covers.

- **Attestation front-running, confirmed real via a working PoC, not fixed in this repo.** The signed price attestation `StocksCurve`'s constructor verifies (`keccak256(abi.encodePacked(factory, stockToken, price, priceTimestamp))`) binds to none of (`msg.sender`, a nonce, a chain id) -- a bearer credential. `StocksLaunchFactory.createCurve`'s replay guard is keyed the same way (`stockToken, price, priceTimestamp` only), consuming the same slot regardless of who calls. Since the attestation must be submitted as public transaction calldata to be used at all, anyone who observes it (in the mempool, or simply anyone the legitimate requester shares it with) can submit it first, permanently consuming that exact attestation and leaving the intended creator's own identical submission to revert with `AttestationAlreadyUsed`. Proven directly (`test/StocksLaunchFactory.attestationFrontrun.t.sol`): an attacker submits the creator's own signed attestation first and succeeds; the creator's subsequent call with the identical attestation reverts. This does NOT let the attacker steal funds or an allocation -- the new TST's entire supply goes straight to the new curve contract, not to `msg.sender` -- so front-running confers no direct token or fee benefit. The real harm is griefing (the creator's transaction reverts, wasting their gas, and they need a fresh, differently-timestamped attestation to retry) and impersonation/brand-squatting (the attacker picks their own `name`/`symbol`/`metadataURI` for a curve tied to the same real `stockToken`+`price` the creator intended, which a user navigating by stock rather than by curve address could mistake for the legitimate launch). A robust fix needs either the off-chain price-signer service updated to bind the intended creator's address into what it signs, or a commit-reveal submission flow added to the frontend's own submission logic -- both outside this repo's scope, and the exact same category as this document's own already-documented, deliberately deferred EIP-712 domain-separation gap ("would need the off-chain signer service upgraded in lockstep, outside this repo"). Documented here as a concrete, proven recommendation for the next generation rather than attempted as a partial, contracts-only patch that can't fully close it alone.

- **TWAMM interval-boundary trades, assessed: not a first-party bug.** TWAMM's batched execution (`TWAMM._executeTWAMMOrders`/`_advanceTimestampForSinglePoolSell`) catches up pending orders in discrete steps at `expirationInterval` boundaries, triggered opportunistically by whoever's ordinary swap next calls `_safeTwammExecute` after a boundary has passed -- meaning an attacker can, in principle, be that triggering swap themselves (manipulating price and forcing the batched fill to execute at that manipulated price in one transaction), then reverse their position immediately after, a classic sandwich pattern. This is a well-known, general, inherent property of any TWAMM-style batched-order primitive (discussed as an accepted trade-off in the original Paradigm TWAMM design this vendored contract is built on), not a first-party coding mistake in this repo, and "fixing" it at the contract level would mean rewriting or replacing an established, external primitive rather than patching a bug. Round 5's already-shipped `MIN_LIQUIDATION_INTERVALS` floor (24) already substantially mitigates the specific treasury-liquidation angle the review raises: spreading any single liquidation across at least 24 separate small chunks means an attacker would need to repeatedly manipulate price 24+ separate times, each with its own real transaction/price-impact cost, to meaningfully extract value across a whole liquidation -- a materially different, much less attractive proposition than manipulating one large, atomic sell. Documented here rather than patched, consistent with how round 7 already treated the closely-related "TWAMM order interval-boundary front-loading" finding for ordinary user orders (I-1/G.3: accepted, vendor-primitive behavior, not fixed).

**Reopened and now fixed: "a range order trades TST and stock without paying the hook fee."** Round 10 closed this as a non-issue, but that reasoning specifically addressed JIT-liquidity fee-*skimming* (a bot temporarily providing liquidity to capture a share of someone else's swap fee) -- inapplicable here since every pool uses `fee: 0`, so there is no native LP fee to skim. Built a real reproduction (`test/StocksHook.rangeOrderFeeBypass.t.sol`) rather than trusting the review's own unverified numbers, and measured the actual magnitude directly: 918,470.63e18 TST deposited into a narrow range entirely above the current price, held while an unrelated ordinary swap (which correctly paid its own full flywheel cost) pushed price through the whole range, then removed -- returned 1,277,542.29e18 stock with **0** reaching the treasury. The identical 918,470.63e18 TST run through an honest `sell()` instead returned only 825,107.89e18 stock, ~35% less, with 73,342.92e18 correctly going to the treasury. A rational actor was strictly better off using the bypass than trading normally.

**Fix:** `beforeAddLiquidity` now reverts (`NonFullRangeLiquidityNotAllowed`) on any position whose `tickLower`/`tickUpper` aren't exactly full-range for the pool's tick spacing. This protocol's own design never wanted third-party concentrated liquidity in the first place -- the graduator seeds exactly one permanent full-range position and nothing else was ever meant to exist alongside it -- so refusing anything narrower closes the mechanism outright rather than trying to price it. A fee-charging alternative was considered and rejected: it would need `afterAddLiquidity`/`afterAddLiquidityReturnDelta` permissions, which are baked into a Uniswap v4 hook's own mined address and are not enabled on this already-deployed hook, so it isn't an option regardless of preference. Full-range liquidity itself (the only kind this protocol's design actually uses) is unaffected, confirmed by a dedicated no-regression test.

One pre-existing test (`test/StocksHook.audit5.t.sol`'s third-party-liquidity/TWAMM-interaction test) used a concentrated range as its test vehicle and was adapted to full-range, since the fix makes concentrated third-party liquidity impossible and the test's actual purpose (does liquidity fully entering and exiting, with a TWAMM order outstanding, ever brick the pool) never depended on the position being concentrated.

**Verification:** `forge build` clean; the new regression file (3 tests) and the full non-solvency `StocksHook*` suite (48 tests across 26 suites, including the adapted `audit5.t.sol` test) all pass, 0 failed. ## Round 14 (2026-09-30, directed adversarial testing of the liquidation/redemption/staking/TWAMM boundary, not from an external report)

A deliberate, hand-directed attack pass against the three-way interaction between `StocksStaking` (redemption, staking rewards, treasury liquidation) and TWAMM (order submission, execution, claiming) -- the specific combination an earlier round's own broad randomized invariant campaign (`StocksSystem.invariant.t.sol`, 60,000 random handler calls) already covers statistically, but a targeted, worst-case-seeking pass covers differently: hand-picked scenarios a real attacker holding real TVL would actually try, not chance.

**Six scenarios checked and confirmed safe, each with a new dedicated regression test (`test/StocksStaking.liquidationAdversarial.t.sol`):**
- An opposing TWAMM order (buying stock with TST, the reverse of the treasury's own sell-stock-for-TST liquidation order) can never make the treasury's realized buyback worse -- TWAMM's order-netting only helps both matched parties. Measured: the treasury burned *more* TST with an opposing order present than without. *(Still true after round 25, which adds that the order's owner can take part of that gain back by pushing spot before each catch-up when the slice is large.)*
- A liquidation large enough to zero out the remaining reward stream (already-documented, accepted design: governance may redirect the unvested stream into a buyback) never touches what a staker had *already* earned before the liquidation -- proven bit-for-bit exact, and still fully claimable afterward.
- Redeeming the entire non-burned TST supply while a liquidation order is still mid-flight (partially executed, unclaimed) works correctly: pays out real value, never exceeds the treasury's actual balance, and the still-live order settles normally afterward.
- TWAMM catch-up execution cannot be sandwiched: `_safeTwammExecute` (and the vendored `executeTWAMMOrders` it wraps) runs at the very start of `beforeSwap`/`beforeAddLiquidity`/`beforeRemoveLiquidity`, strictly BEFORE that same call's own price impact applies -- so whoever's transaction finally triggers a pending catch-up can never also be the one who moved price first. Proven empirically: a lone push landing immediately after its own triggered catch-up left the treasury no worse off than repeated pushes whose impact carries into later chunks (in fact slightly better, since the lump case's catch-up completes before any manipulation from that same call can apply).
- Liquidating while rewards are paused, then unpausing, keeps every part of the accounting sound (`_rewardClockNow()`'s frozen clock handles this correctly by design).
- Liquidating in a pool where nobody has ever staked (`totalStaked == 0` throughout) hits no division-by-zero and correctly commits essentially the entire treasury, since nothing is vested to anyone to protect.

**One real finding: a gas-griefing gap in TWAMM order submission and claiming, found and fixed.** `TWAMM._submitOrder` and `TWAMM.sync` both call `executeTWAMMOrders(key)` directly, with none of `_safeTwammExecute`'s fail-open protection that `beforeSwap`/`beforeAddLiquidity`/`beforeRemoveLiquidity` already have. Regular user orders carry no minimum size (only `SellRateCannotBeZero`, satisfiable with a handful of wei), so an attacker can cheaply submit many orders staggered across distinct interval boundaries to inflate the gas cost of a LATER `submitOrder`/`liquidateTreasury` (governance-only) or `sync`/`claimLiquidatedTst` (permissionless) call that has to catch up across the whole window.

Measured directly against the real vendored TWAMM, not estimated: baseline `liquidateTreasury` costs 288,635 gas; with 20 orders staggered one per hour across 20 distinct expirations, 1,217,228 gas (~46,429 extra gas per segment); `claimLiquidatedTst` shows the identical pattern (408,218 baseline, 1,461,418 with the same 20-order backlog, ~52,660/segment). Checked whether this scaling holds at larger N rather than assuming linearity: at n=60 the per-segment cost is 43,259 gas -- confirmed not worse than linear (if anything, slightly better, some fixed overhead amortizing), validating a rough extrapolation that a few hundred staggered orders (each costing the attacker only ~1e6 wei of TST plus cheap L2 submission gas) could plausibly push a single call toward a realistic block gas limit.

Not a fund-safety issue and not a permanent block, confirmed before treating it as anything more than an operational gap: the backlog is never lost, only deferred, and the vendored `executeTWAMMOrders(key, targetTimestamp)` overload is already public and callable by anyone. Proven directly that walking it down in small, separate steps (10 steps of ~2 hours each, each under 2,000,000 gas) fully defuses it, after which the expensive call returns to its exact normal baseline cost.

**Fix:** `StocksHook.pumpTwammBacklog(key, stepSeconds, maxSteps)` -- a new, permissionless function that walks a pool's backlog down in bounded, interval-sized steps within ONE transaction, using the same already-public `executeTWAMMOrders(key, targetTimestamp)` anyone could already call repeatedly by hand. Grants no new capability; it only closes the *operational* gap the proof above exposed -- doing this manually needs one transaction per step, exactly the friction a griefer is counting on someone not bothering with. Verified: one call fully clears a 20-order staggered backlog (`liquidateTreasury` cost afterward: 288,679 gas, matching the untouched baseline of 288,635 almost exactly); a cheap no-op when nothing is outstanding; reverts clearly (`NothingToPump`) on degenerate inputs (zero step size, zero max steps, or an uninitialized pool); stops as soon as caught up rather than iterating a caller-supplied `maxSteps` regardless of actual need.

**Follow-up, worst-case severity refined rather than assumed:** the original measurement used same-direction staggered orders only. Checked whether ALTERNATING order direction (forcing TWAMM's order-netting, `_exhaustMatchedOrders`, to actually run on every segment instead of trivially no-opping) costs more gas per segment -- it does, substantially: 82,443 gas/segment versus the same-direction figure's 46,429, essentially double. This revises the rough extrapolation downward to roughly 360 staggered orders (not ~640) to approach a 30M block gas limit, meaning the underlying gap was cheaper for a real attacker to exploit than the initial same-direction-only measurement suggested. The fix's own mechanism doesn't depend on any assumption about per-segment cost, but this was confirmed directly rather than assumed too: `pumpTwammBacklog` fully defuses the worse alternating-direction backlog as well (`liquidateTreasury` cost afterward: 274,380 gas, at or below the untouched baseline).

**Also checked and confirmed safe: a same-direction COMPETING order (a third party selling the same asset the treasury's own liquidation order is selling, concurrently, rather than netting against it).** Initial measurement showed an alarming ~8% gap between the treasury's and the competitor's realized rates for identically-sized, identically-timed orders -- investigated rather than dismissed or loosened away. Root cause was a test measurement bug, not a contract issue: both orders have `feeIsTst=true`, so each order's protocol fee is burned separately from its net proceeds; the treasury's own liquidation additionally burns its own net proceeds (so "total TST burned by the liquidation" is already gross), while a plain user's claim only pays them the net amount (their fee was also burned, just never reaching their wallet). Comparing "total burned" against "wallet receipt only" compared gross to net, not a real unfairness. Corrected to compare gross-to-gross (adding back each party's own burned fee, read from the real `TwammOrderProceedsFeeCharged` event each `sync()` call emits): the two rates match to 15 significant figures, confirming TWAMM's pro-rata splitting between concurrent same-direction orders is genuinely, exactly fair.

**Verification:** 25/25 tests in the new adversarial file (the six original safe-scenario checks, the same-direction-competitor fairness check, the gas-cost measurements at n=20/n=60 and the alternating-direction variant, and six dedicated tests for the fix and its worst-case coverage), the full non-solvency `StocksHook*` suite (53/53), and the 60,000-call `StocksSystem.invariant` campaign all pass clean. Separately re-run at 8x scale (1000 runs x 500 depth = 500,000 calls, temporarily, not a committed config change) specifically hunting for anything the directed tests missed: 0 failures, all 11 invariants held.

Every test above ran against a freshly-deployed local `PoolManager`, never the real, live Ink one this fix will actually run against in production. Verified separately (`test/StocksStaking.realHookLiquidation.t.sol`): reproduced the same staggered-order gas-griefing setup and the fix end to end against the real fork, through a real curve, real graduation, and a real governance-pranked `liquidateTreasury` call. ## Round 19 (2026-09-30, user-directed pivot: "now do business logic")

A deliberate shift in methodology, not just target: every round before this one asks "can the mechanism be broken or exploited?" This round asks a different question -- "does the mechanism actually do what it's for, and for whom?" -- against the project's own core thesis: Stocks.ink positions TST as the inverse of a Digital Asset Treasury, where backing is supposed to grow from the token's own organic trading volume through the flywheel, rather than a static or self-dealt crypto reserve.

**Method:** simulated 180 days of realistic, non-adversarial trading (alternating buy/sell direction, sized modestly relative to the real pool's own depth, spread across real elapsed calendar time -- not one giant trade, not an adversarial sandwich) and measured, precisely, who actually captures the flywheel's real accrued value.

**Finding, confirmed and quantified, not assumed: the flywheel's growth is captured overwhelmingly by stakers, not by passive, non-staking holders, and by a margin far out of proportion to the staked share of supply.** `StocksStaking._notifyReward()` treats ANY stock inflow to the contract -- organic sell-side flywheel fees included, exactly like a direct donation -- as a reward stream owed entirely to CURRENTLY STAKED tokens (`rewardPerToken()`'s denominator is `totalStaked`, not `nonBurnedSupply()`). A passive holder's only claim on that same growing balance is `redeem()`, which can only ever draw on `balance - earned`, i.e. whatever is NOT already earmarked for stakers.

Measured directly over the 180-day simulation: with only **7.08%** of circulating supply staked, the staked fraction captured **83.49%** of the real flywheel accrual from the same organic trading volume -- roughly **11.8x** its proportional share of supply. The passive 92.92% of holders shared the remaining 16.51%. Passive holders DID see real, positive redeemable growth over the window (not zero -- the thesis is not simply false), and TST burn from buy-side fees benefits every holder equally regardless of staking status (a real, universal, un-skewed channel). But the "backing grows with volume" framing, taken at face value without the staking qualification, meaningfully understates how concentrated that growth actually is.

**Not a fund-safety issue.** `redeemableStock()` still floors correctly at zero under every condition, and `redeem()` can never pay out into insolvency -- this was independently re-confirmed by the same 128,000-call `StocksStaking` solvency invariant campaign this test ran alongside. This is a genuine, quantified fact about the protocol's own incentive design, not a bug: it is also, in one reading, exactly what a staking-reward mechanism is FOR -- rewarding the specific behavior (locking up TST) the protocol wants to encourage, rather than diluting that reward equally across everyone including holders who contribute nothing by simply holding. Whether the current 11.8x concentration is the intended degree, and whether user-facing copy describing "backing that grows with volume" adequately conveys that the real benefit is staking-gated, are product and communication questions outside this document's own scope -- recorded here as a concrete, measured input to that decision rather than left as an unstated assumption.

## Round 20 (2026-09-30, continuing "keep testing the business logic to find vulnerabilities" -- a real, severe finding)

Following directly from round 19's own finding (the flywheel gives every holder a strong, rational incentive to stake, since stakers capture roughly 11.8x their proportional share of value): what happens to *governance* as that rational incentive plays out and staking participation grows?

**Two already-separately-documented facts, never previously combined and tested together:**
1. Staked TST sits inside `StocksStaking`, which never delegates its own held tokens anywhere -- grepped directly, zero `delegate` calls in the entire contract. Staked TST therefore carries **zero voting power**, confirmed structurally, not assumed.
2. `StocksGovernor.quorum()`'s denominator is circulating supply (`getPastTotalSupply(timepoint) - burned`), which **counts staked TST** -- it isn't burned, just held in a different contract. `QUORUM_NUMERATOR` is fixed at 10% of that, forever: `updateQuorumNumerator()` is a hard `pure` override that reverts unconditionally, for any caller, including the governor's own address -- already known to be immutable from the existing dedicated regression suite (`test/StocksGovernor.quorumLock.regression.t.sol`), but never tested against the specific question this round asks: can that fixed threshold become mathematically unreachable purely through staking dynamics, with nobody doing anything wrong?

**Measured directly (`test/StocksBusinessLogic.quorumBrick.t.sol`), not assumed:**

| Staked share of circulating supply | Can a lone remaining voter, with 100% unanimous support from every non-staked holder, still pass a routine proposal? |
|---|---|
| 50% | Yes |
| 80% | Yes |
| **89%** | **No** |
| 91% | No |
| 99% | No |

At 89% staked, the maximum possible "For" votes (6.09e25, every last unit of non-staked supply voting unanimously) falls short of the fixed quorum requirement (7.54e25) -- not a close call decided by turnout, a structural impossibility. The precise crossover sits between 80% and 89% in this scenario, consistent with the clean theoretical boundary at exactly 90% (100% minus the fixed 10% quorum numerator). Below the threshold (80%), the identical unanimous-vote mechanism genuinely passes, confirming the failure at 89%+ is real and not a test-harness artifact.

**Confirmed directly: there is no recovery path once this state is reached.** `updateQuorumNumerator()` reverts unconditionally for every caller tested, including a direct call from the governor's own address -- no proposal, however constructed, could ever lower quorum to recover, because passing that proposal would itself require reaching the now-unreachable quorum. This is a genuine deadlock, not merely an inconvenience.

**Severity, stated plainly: real and structural, not a fund-theft exploit.** Nobody's tokens are stolen and `StocksStaking`'s own reward/redemption solvency stays completely intact throughout (independently reconfirmed by the unaffected 128,000-call solvency invariant campaign, run in the same regression pass) -- `liquidateTreasury`, `setRewardsPaused`, `setRewardsDuration`, and every other `onlyGovernor` lever simply become permanently unusable once enough of the supply is staked, with the treasury and staking mechanics otherwise continuing to function normally on whatever settings were last in place. But this is a genuine governance-availability risk, and a notable one specifically because it is **directly created by the protocol's own core incentive design succeeding at its own stated goal** -- the more effectively the flywheel encourages staking (round 19's own finding), the closer the protocol moves toward this exact threshold. Recorded here as a concrete, quantified input to a real design decision (for example: delegating the staking contract's own held tokens back to stakers proportionally so staked TST retains voting power, or excluding staked TST from quorum's denominator specifically) rather than left as an unquantified theoretical concern.

**Verification:** new test file (`test/StocksBusinessLogic.quorumBrick.t.sol`, 2 tests) passes with the real numbers above; full `StocksAuditR2Governance`/`StocksQuorumBrick`/`StocksGovernor` regression (52/52 across 8 suites, including the pre-existing quorum-immutability test suite) unaffected. No source change -- this documents and quantifies a design characteristic rather than fixing one, and is presented as an input to a decision rather than a verdict.

## Round 22 (2026-10-01, user-directed: "run whole audit on the whole project smart contracts, check all possible cases and make it break")

A fresh full-project pass over all 12 first-party contracts. Its first target was deliberately the newest and least-proven code in the repo -- round 21's own four fixes, shipped the previous day -- on the principle that yesterday's patch is today's attack surface. That choice paid off: the most substantive finding of this round is a real defect in round 21's own fix. Baseline before any change: **106 suites, 619 tests, 0 failed.** New file `test/StocksAuditR22.adversarial.t.sol` (8 tests), all passing, plus 65/65 on every regression-risk suite afterwards.

**1. (Low, REAL, in this repo's own round-21 fix -- found and fixed here.) The `SeedWouldBeUnreachable` buy-guard was necessary but not sufficient, and its source comment named a recovery path that does not work.**

Round 21 added a guard refusing any buy that would push `remaining` below the graduator's 1%-of-supply seed floor (`minSeed` = 10,000,000e18). Its stated reasoning was that `tstToSeed` is always strictly LESS than `remaining`, so capping `remaining` suffices. That gets the direction right and the sufficiency exactly backwards: *because* `tstToSeed < remaining`, pinning `remaining` to exactly `minSeed` leaves the seed BELOW `minSeed`. Measured directly on the real fixture before fixing: buying down to `remaining == 10,000,000e18` produced `tstToSeed = 9,875,000e18` against the graduator's required `10,000,000e18`, and `graduate()` reverted `SeedTooSmall()` -- at a state the guard had explicitly permitted a buy to reach.

Not a permanent brick (selling raises `remaining`, which on this side of the curve raises `tstToSeed` with it), so severity is Low. But the guard's own comment asserted the cure was *more buying*, which is wrong twice over: buying shrinks `remaining` rather than leaving it alone, and at the boundary buying is refused outright. That comment has been corrected rather than quietly deleted, since it was the reasoning error that produced the gap.

Fixed by additionally checking the real seed formula, but only on the LATE side of the curve. `tstToSeed` as a function of `remaining` is a downward parabola peaking at `remaining = CURVE_SUPPLY/2`, so it dips below `minSeed` in two separate places: near `CURVE_SUPPLY` (early, where the cure genuinely IS more buying -- this is the exact case round 21's rejected first draft broke) and near zero (late, where more buying is precisely the danger). Restricting the seed check to `remaining < CURVE_SUPPLY/2` catches the late case without touching the early one. Writing `n` for the overshoot as a multiple of the graduation target, the guard's new boundary solves `9n² - 234n + 1 <= 0`, i.e. `n ∈ [0.0043, 25.99]` -- which lands exactly on the **~26x threshold round 13 independently documented** from a completely different direction, an unplanned cross-check that the math is right. Measured floors on the fixture: guard's `minSeed` 10,000,000e18 vs. the true graduatable floor 10,130,000e18 (1.3% higher). The guarantee is now clean: **any buy the guard permits leaves a curve that can actually graduate.**

**2. (Informational, structural.) `graduate()`'s `soldOut` condition is provably dead code.**

The curve is a constant product, so `(virtualStockReserve + realStockCollected) * remaining` is invariant. With `V0 = T/3` and `R0 = CURVE_SUPPLY` (T = graduationStockTarget), reaching `soldOut` (R <= 3% of CURVE_SUPPLY) forces `V >= 11.1 * T`, hence `realStockCollected >= 10.78 * T` -- an order of magnitude past what `targetReached` tests for. The two conditions are not independent: **`soldOut` implies `targetReached`, always**, so `soldOut` can never be the deciding factor. Measured across four prices spanning eight orders of magnitude ($0.01 to $1,000,000 a share): `collected / target` came out at **10.78x at every single one**, invariant in price exactly as the algebra predicts.

Consequence for the record: round 21 lowered `SOLDOUT_THRESHOLD_BPS` 9,900 -> 9,700 as part of AuditAgent finding #8, framed as resolving a conflict between that threshold and the seed floor. On this analysis that change was harmless but **cosmetic** -- the branch it tunes cannot independently fire. The real defect finding #8 closed was the missing buy()-side cap, which applies to every graduation path. Noted so the repo's own account of that fix stays accurate rather than overstated.

**3. (Medium, NEW -- escalates round 20.) Permanently locked pool liquidity is non-voting but counts in quorum's denominator.**

Round 20 established that staked TST has zero voting power while still sitting in `quorum()`'s denominator, and put the resulting deadlock threshold at roughly 89% of circulating supply staked. That measurement missed a second and larger block of permanently non-voting supply that exists from the moment a pool opens: the graduation seed itself. `quorum()` divides `getPastTotalSupply(timepoint) - burned`, i.e. everything except the burn address. At graduation `tstToSeed` is transferred into the Uniswap v4 PoolManager, a singleton that never calls `delegate()` -- and unlike staked TST, which a holder can unstake at any time to recover its vote, this liquidity is locked permanently by design (it is the platform's core "liquidity locked forever" promise; `StocksGraduator` exposes no withdrawal path). It is therefore unconditionally and irreversibly non-voting, yet it sits in the quorum denominator for the life of the protocol.

Measured on a real graduated curve: circulating 750,000,000e18; **pool-held 149,999,899...e18 = 1,999 bps (~20%) of circulating**, with `getVotes(poolManager)` confirmed at exactly 0; holders 600,000,000e18; quorum 75,000,000e18. Holders must therefore cover the entire quorum alone, so the deadlock point arrives once a further **7,000 bps (70%) of circulating** becomes non-voting -- materially below round 20's 89% figure, and that is before accounting for ordinary ERC20Votes behaviour, where a holder who never calls `delegate()` is also non-voting while still counted in the denominator. With `updateQuorumNumerator()` reverting unconditionally for every caller (round 20), there is still no recovery path. No funds are at risk; this is purely governance availability, and is recorded as a design-decision input (delegate the pool's and staking contract's holdings back proportionally, or exclude them from the denominator) rather than a verdict.

**4. (Near-miss, verified safe, now regression-locked.) The launch path cannot be bricked through `TokenMetadataRegistry`, and two single lines are the only reason.**

`setMetadataURI` is fully permissionless and PERMANENT (`AlreadySet()` on any second write), and `createCurve` calls it on the TST token it has just deployed at a trivially predictable CREATE address. If an attacker could claim that entry first, `createCurve` would revert at its very last step -- and because a reverted call does not advance the factory's nonce, the SAME address stays poisoned on every retry, permanently bricking every future launch from that factory for the price of one transaction. Two things prevent it: `setMetadataURI` rejects an address with no code (`TokenHasNoCode`), so the entry cannot be claimed before the token exists; and the only attacker-influenced external call in the window between the token gaining code and the registry write -- `stockToken.decimals()` from StocksCurve's constructor -- is a **staticcall**, so it cannot be used to claim the entry during that window either.

Both are now pinned by tests, because each is a single line a well-meaning future change could remove. The second test uses a hand-rolled stock token whose `decimals()` is deliberately NOT `view` so it can genuinely attempt the nested registry write (OpenZeppelin's ERC20 declares `decimals()` as `view`, and Solidity refuses to loosen an override's mutability -- it rejects a state-modifying `call` even when written in inline assembly inside a view function, which is itself worth knowing). A control case proves the attack contract works when reached outside a static context, so the pass is the boundary holding rather than a broken attacker.

**5. (Verified safe, quantified.) The new buy-guard is not a usable denial-of-service, and an extreme donation cannot brick graduation.**

The guard only binds on a curve that has collected **25.06x** its graduation target, at which point graduation has been available for a very long time and anyone can end the situation permissionlessly in one call. Griefing a victim's buy is possible in principle but cost the attacker **412,967,320,261,437,908,471 wei of stock, 25.81x the graduation target**, with the victim losing nothing but gas (balance asserted unchanged, the revert being atomic). Separately, `_graduate()` seeds the stock side from the curve's live balance (the accepted H-2 trade-off), and donated stock can never be withdrawn -- `sell()` pays out of the accounting figure and `skim()` sweeps only stray TST -- so a donation large enough to overflow the `SafeCast.toUint160` on `sqrtPriceX96` or the `toUint128` on liquidity would be an unrecoverable brick rather than a bad price. Tested with the largest donation the fixture can physically make, **170,141,183,460,469,231,699,687,303,715,884,105,727 wei** (the whole of `type(uint128).max`): graduation still completes and the curve retains no stock. The bound is not reachable with any realistic token -- the opening price scales as `sqrt(poolStock/poolTst)` and `poolTst` is floored near 1e25 by the seed minimum, so reaching uint160 would need upwards of 1e63 wei, about 25 orders of magnitude past a uint128-supply token.

**Also re-derived and confirmed unchanged (no action):** `sell()`'s round-21 receipt measurement keeps accounting and real balance exactly equal even when the seller moves its own proceeds mid-transfer; the round-21 name-length cap is complete (`symbol` reaches no capped context, `string.concat` appears exactly once in `src/`, and the name flows unmodified through a single creation path); `skim()` cannot underflow; `_graduate()`'s `tstExcess` subtraction cannot underflow (balance = remaining + RESERVED_SUPPLY always exceeds `tstToSeed < remaining`); and the ignored return value on the reward ping in `_routeStockFee` is load-bearing, since during `liquidateTreasury` the nested `notifyRewardAmount()` is correctly refused by the shared reentrancy guard and the explicit `_notifyReward()` at the end of that function covers it.

**1. (Low, latent.) Read-only reentrancy on the REDEMPTION RATE -- the one shape round 10's read-only pass did not look at.**

*(Fixed in round 26: `redeem()` now pays out before it burns, so the mid-call rate reads low instead of high. The description below is of the code as it was.)*

Round 10 did check read-only reentrancy, but only against `StocksHook.getReserves()` and the price accumulator, and correctly concluded those are live Uniswap state with no on-chain consumer. It never examined `StocksStaking.quoteRedeem` / `redeemableStock`, which is the far more dangerous shape and precisely the one that made Curve's `get_virtual_price` the canonical $73M case: an independently COMPUTED ratio of two values the contract itself mutates, published through a view that no guard protects -- OpenZeppelin's `nonReentrant` does not cover view functions.

`redeem()` moves the numerator and the denominator of that ratio in two SEPARATE external calls:

```
tstToken.safeTransferFrom(msg.sender, BURN_ADDRESS, tstAmount);  // denominator drops here
stockToken.safeTransfer(msg.sender, stockOut);                   // numerator drops here
if (protocolCut > 0) stockToken.safeTransfer(protocol, protocolCut);
```

Between them the supply has already fallen by the full `tstAmount` while the treasury still holds stock that is about to leave, so the published rate is transiently higher than any rate ever actually settled -- higher than before the redemption AND higher than after it. Measured with a pre-transfer callback on a 10%-of-supply redemption: quoted **99.999...e18** mid-call against a settled **90.799...e18**, an overstatement of **1,013 bps (10.13%)**, versus **90.0e18** before. A post-transfer (ERC777 `tokensReceived`) callback still reads **90.999...e18** against the same settled 90.8, so the window is inherent to the ordering rather than an artifact of where a callback sits.

Nothing is stolen from this protocol directly; the loss lands on whoever trusts the inflated quote -- a lending market valuing TST collateral, a vault, an aggregator. **Not currently reachable:** every attested xStock wrapper is a plain ERC20/ERC4626 with no transfer callback (the existing 723-wrapper sweep confirms it), `TSTToken` is a plain ERC20Votes whose transfers make no external calls, and the burn goes to a dead address -- a control case in the same file confirms that with a plain token no intermediate state is observable at all. So this needs either a future attested stock token with callbacks or a future code change that introduces one.

Left documented rather than fixed because the mitigation is a genuine trade-off for the owner to weigh: either move the TST burn after the stock transfers (so the denominator never leads the numerator, at the cost of paying out before burning) or publish the rate behind a reentrancy-status check in the views. Recorded with a working PoC so the decision is made against measured numbers rather than a hypothetical.

**2. (Informational.) No deadline or expiry on any user-facing value transfer.**

`grep -rn "deadline" src/` returns nothing. `StocksCurve.buy`, `StocksCurve.sell` and `StocksStaking.redeem` each take a slippage bound and nothing else, so a transaction held in the mempool -- or deliberately withheld by a builder -- can execute at any later time at whatever rate prevails then.

Graded Informational rather than Medium, with the reason recorded precisely rather than hand-waved: the signed `minStockOut`/`minTstOut` still bounds the outcome in absolute token terms whenever the trade lands, so there is no path to filling below the floor the user actually signed. What a deadline would additionally buy is protection against a stale order filling above that floor but far below the then-current market rate. For redemption specifically the exposure is unusually small, because the settled rate is monotonically non-decreasing for holders who stay (independently asserted in the same file), so a delayed redemption fills at a rate at least as good as the one quoted -- demonstrated by executing a year-stale redemption against its original quote rather than argued.

**Verification:** 4/4 in the new file; no source changes this round, so no regression surface. Round 22's baseline (106 suites / 619 tests / 0 failed) stands unchanged.

## Round 24 (2026-10-02, continuation of the full-project pass: three candidate fixes, two kept, one rejected on measurement)

Three source changes were drafted this round. Each was then held to the same standard as an external finding: a regression test that fails without the change and passes with it, or the change does not ship. Two survived. The third was shown by its own test not to do what its comment claimed, and was reverted.

**1. (Medium, REAL, fixed.) A reward-period restart could SHORTEN the vesting of the leftover stream after governance lowered `rewardsDuration`.**

The earlier F-2 (reward sniping) fix made a material inflow (at least 1/100 of what is left to stream) restart the reward period, spreading `inflow + leftover` over a full `rewardsDuration` so a latecomer cannot capture a compressed stream. That is only a defence while `rewardsDuration` is at least the time left on the running period. The constructor value always is, but `setRewardsDuration` lets governance lower it to as little as 1 day while a longer period is still running. From then on a restart packed the whole leftover into the new, shorter window -- and the restart trigger is permissionless and costs only a donation of 1% of the leftover.

Measured on day 1 of a 30-day, 1,000-stock stream after the duration was lowered to 1 day: a stake 100x the honest one joined, donated ~9.7 stock to force the restart, held one day and claimed. Without the fix `periodFinish` moved from 29 days out to 1 day out and the whole ~967 of leftover vested in that day, ~99% of it to the newcomer. With the fix it claimed **33** -- one day's pro-rata share of a 29-day stream.

Fixed in `StocksStaking._notifyReward()`: a restart now runs over `max(rewardsDuration, remaining)`. The governed duration still takes over as soon as the running period ends, and raising the duration behaves exactly as before. New file `test/StocksStaking.restartNeverShortens.regression.t.sol` (10 tests, two of them fuzzed over every governable duration). Negative control run and recorded: with the one line reverted, 4 of the 10 fail, including the fuzzed time-share bound (145.3e18 earned against a 72.3e18 bound); the other 6 are the cases the fix is meant to leave alone, and pass either way. Not fund-unsafe -- solvency never depended on the period length -- but a real, cheap redistribution from existing stakers to a latecomer, reachable through an ordinary governance action that looks harmless.

**2. (Low, REAL, fixed.) `quorum()` used the burn balance from proposal CREATION, not from the vote snapshot.**

`StocksGovernor` subtracts burned TST from the quorum denominator and pins that figure per snapshot timepoint so it cannot drift once voting has begun. It was recording it inside `_propose`, a whole `votingDelay` before the snapshot it is keyed by. TST burns continuously in that gap (buy-side costs, liquidation claims, redemptions), so the recorded figure credited circulating supply with tokens that were already gone by the snapshot and set quorum ABOVE its true value. The error is bounded by one voting delay's worth of burn, but it always falls in the unsafe direction for a threshold that is immutable and, per rounds 20 and 22, already carries a large block of permanently non-voting supply.

It cannot be read inside `quorum()` (a view). Fixed by recording it in `_castVote` instead: voting is only possible once the proposal is Active, so the first vote pins the figure at or after the snapshot; until then `quorum()` reads the live balance. A reverted vote (wrong state, already voted) rolls the recording back with it. Any residual error now makes quorum slightly LOWER rather than higher, and lowering it deliberately means actually burning TST. `test/StocksAuditR2.governorEdges.t.sol` was rewritten to assert the new guarantee end to end: a burn before the snapshot is counted, the first vote pins the figure, and neither a later burn nor a vote on a second proposal sharing the timepoint can move it.

**3. (Rejected on measurement, no source change.) A settlement top-up loop for fee-on-transfer tokens in `StocksGraduator`.**

The candidate: have `_settleCurrency` measure what the PoolManager actually received and top up the shortfall in a bounded loop, so that a token taking a cut on transfer could still graduate. The existing coverage (`StocksGraduator.audit3.t.sol`) could not judge it, because it only asserts a bare `vm.expectRevert()`, which passes whatever the reason. A probe with a pinned revert selector settled it: with the loop in place both the fee-on-transfer stock case and the fee-on-transfer TST case still reverted, now with `ERC20InsufficientBalance` on the very first top-up. The graduator sizes its position from its entire balance, so after the first settlement transfer it holds only rounding dust and has nothing to top up with. Same outcome, more code, and a source comment asserting the opposite.

Making it genuinely work would mean sizing liquidity below what the graduator holds, and that is the wrong goal regardless: `StocksHook._takeExact` requires exact receipts on every swap that charges the flywheel cost, so a fee-on-transfer stock that DID graduate would have its seed locked permanently in a pool whose swaps revert. Failing at graduation, while holders can still sell back to the curve, is the safe behaviour. The change was reverted and the fail-closed behaviour is now pinned precisely in `test/StocksGraduator.feeOnTransferSettlement.t.sol` (3 tests): both token sides revert with v4-core's own `CurrencyNotSettled()`, the curve keeps every wei, and a plain-token graduation conserves both tokens exactly across pool, hook reserve and swept dust. Unchanged status: not reachable today, since no attestable token charges a transfer cut (723-wrapper sweep).

**Verification:** full suite after the changes, every file including both solvency invariant campaigns and the real-Ink fork tests: **112 suites / 648 tests / 0 failed** (round 22's baseline was 106 / 619; the 723-wrapper full sweep is opt-in and was not re-run, being unrelated to these changes). ## Round 25 (2026-10-02, user-directed: a fresh contract-by-contract re-audit, code and business logic, on a more capable model)

A full line-by-line read of all 12 first-party contracts and the vendored TWAMM, in lifecycle order (launch factory, token, curve, graduator, hook, TWAMM, staking, governor, the three spawner factories, registry, pool view), deliberately without leaning on earlier rounds' conclusions: each earlier "verified safe" claim that the read touched was treated as a hypothesis to re-derive. No source change this round. No new High or Medium. One new Low, found by combining two earlier conclusions that had each been tested alone; one external assumption that earlier rounds relied on and never tested, now tested; and a list of what was re-derived and held.

**1. (Low, NEW.) An opposing TWAMM order plus a price push just before each catch-up extracts value from a large treasury liquidation. Two earlier conclusions do not compose.**

Round 2 concluded that sandwiching a liquidation always loses because of the 10% cost on each leg. Round 14 concluded that an opposing TWAMM order can never make the treasury's liquidation worse. Both are true as tested, and they were never tested together.

`TWAMM._exhaustMatchedOrders` nets opposing orders against each other at the pool's SPOT price at the start of each interval, with no price impact on the matched amount and no bound on how far spot sits from where it was a block earlier. An attacker holding an opposing order (selling TST into the treasury's stock) therefore only needs spot to be high for the instant the interval is caught up: buy TST one block before the interval boundary, trigger the catch-up at the boundary (anyone can, `executeTWAMMOrders` is public), sell the TST back. The push pays its ~19% round trip on the PUSH size, but the gain is collected on the whole MATCHED size, so the per-leg cost stops being a defence once the matched amount is large relative to the pool.

Measured against the real stack (`test/StocksAuditR25.matchedPriceManipulation.t.sol`): pool stock depth ~42, a 24-interval liquidation, an attacker holding 30M TST (worth 8.6 stock at spot) with an order spanning the first two intervals. "Slice" is the liquidation's hourly amount as a share of the pool's stock.

| Slice | Honest opposing order earns | Order + push earns | Treasury TST burned: no counter-order / honest / push |
|---|---|---|---|
| ~49% | 11.29 stock | 20.95 stock | 134.8M / 164.5M / 157.7M |
| ~20% | 8.49 stock | 9.20 stock | 120.5M / 149.4M / 141.9M |
| ~10% | 7.71 stock | 6.65 stock | 102.4M / 129.5M / 124.7M |

So the push pays from roughly a 20% hourly slice upwards and loses below it. Round 14's claim survives in its literal form: even the manipulated counter-order leaves the treasury better off than no counter-order at all. What does not survive is the reading that a counter-order is therefore harmless: the treasury burns 4-5% less TST than it would against the same order left alone, and that difference is what the attacker takes. Round 2's "always loses" holds only for the small slices it tested (orders up to 50% of the pool in total, so about 2% per slice).

Graded Low rather than Medium: it needs a liquidation several times the pool's own stock depth squeezed into the minimum duration, which is a poor liquidation before anyone attacks it (the unassisted order already moves the price heavily), and the treasury is not left worse off than with no counter-party. The lever that removes it is duration, and that is also measured: the same treasury spread over 96 intervals instead of 24 turns the same push from a gain into a loss.

Not fixed in source. An on-chain cap on the slice would have to read the pool's reserves at execution time, which is the very quantity the attacker moves, and `MIN_LIQUIDATION_INTERVALS` cannot be size-aware without it. Recommended instead: the proposal form should show the hourly slice as a share of pool depth and steer the proposer to a duration that keeps it under roughly 5-10%, and the residual-risk list should say plainly that the 24-interval floor bounds front-loading, not manipulation of a large slice.

**2. (Verified safe, previously an untested assumption.) Uniswap's protocol fee being switched on.**

Rounds 3 and 6 classed four TWAMM mutants as equivalent on the grounds that `activeFee` is "provably always 0 ... `setProtocolFee` is never called anywhere in first-party code". True of this repo, not of the PoolManager: `setProtocolFee` belongs to the PoolManager's `protocolFeeController`, which Uniswap's owner can appoint at any time. Read from the live Ink PoolManager this round: `protocolFeeController()` is currently `address(0)`, so the assumption holds today and only by Uniswap's choice. The fee is capped at 0.1% per direction and is charged on every swap's input, including the hook's own TWAMM swaps, and nothing in this protocol can prevent or undo it.

Tested with the fee at its maximum in both directions (`test/StocksAuditR25.protocolFeeOn.t.sol`): a 30-interval liquidation, an opposing order, a competing same-direction order, a pair of short alternating-direction orders dropped in midway, catch-ups that span one and two intervals, and ordinary swaps both ways. Every order was paid in full, the treasury's proceeds were paid in full, and the hook finished holding its graduation reserve untouched (within 185,698 wei of its 100e18 TST, and 1e12+6 of its 1e12 stock wei), marginally better than the fee-off control, because the netted portion of opposing orders never pays the fee it was sized for. Ordinary buys and sells still settle and the flywheel's own split is unchanged (2% of stock in to the protocol and 8% of TST out burned on a buy; 8% of stock out to the treasury on a sell). Those four mutants are therefore no longer "equivalent for this deployment" in the strict sense; they are reachable if Uniswap acts, and the behaviour with the fee on is now covered by a test rather than by an argument.

**3. Re-derived this round and held (no action).**

- *Curve.* The constant-product quote rounds against the trader on both sides; selling the entire `tokensSold` can never pay out more than `realStockCollected`; the opening pool price equals the curve's marginal price exactly (`stockToSeed / tstToSeed == oldVirtualStock / remaining`); buying up the curve and selling into the freshly opened pool returns strictly less than the same sale on the curve, before the 10% cost; a stock donation before graduation raises the opening price at the donor's expense and cannot be recovered at a profit.
- *Launch.* The 22-byte name cap is correct for the vendored OpenZeppelin 5.7.0, whose `EIP712` constructor uses the strict `toShortString` with no storage fallback (re-checked in the library source, since releases with a fallback exist and would have made the cap unnecessary).
- *Graduator.* Only a curve registered by the real factory can call it; nobody else can initialize a pool on this hook; a pre-existing balance on the graduator is swept and never mis-measured. The price computation would overflow only with roughly 8e6 wei of stock or less against the seed, which needs a signed price in the billions of dollars per share.
- *Hook.* Exact-output swaps always revert; the hook's own TWAMM swaps skip its hooks and are charged on proceeds at `sync` instead, with the same split, and there is no path that pays proceeds without going through that override (no cancel or update exists in the vendored contract); the fail-open guard cannot be triggered by a cheap revert (the only deterministic revert inside catch-up needs the virtual price to cross the full-range boundary, which takes many orders of magnitude more of either token than exists); `killHook` is unreachable.
- *TWAMM.* Matched amounts are exchanged at one represented price for both sides, so imprecision at extreme prices moves value between the two order sides and never out of the hook; credited earnings exceed real swap output by rounding only (867,315 wei of TST across the whole mixed scenario above, against a 1e20 reserve).
- *Staking.* The accounting identity `lastNotifiedBalance >= earned + outstanding stream` is preserved by every path (notify, both restart branches including round 24's, redeem's shrink, liquidation, pause and resume); redeemable never includes vested rewards; a liquidation commits exactly the unvested remainder.
- *Governor.* Only three functions anywhere are governor-gated and none takes a recipient; round 24's first-vote recording cannot be pinned early to raise quorum (the burn balance only grows, so the earliest possible pin is the true snapshot value) and a reverted vote records nothing.

**4. Informational, recorded for completeness.**

- TST sent by mistake to the curve after graduation, or to the hook, staking contract or governor, is unrecoverable but still counts in `nonBurnedSupply()` and in quorum's denominator, slightly lowering the redemption rate and raising quorum for everyone else. Self-inflicted and proportional to the mistake.
- The pre-swap 2% cut is sized off the specified input even when a price limit truncates the fill (round 13 made the extreme case revert); a half-filled buy pays an effective 4%.
- `sync`'s cost on proceeds is floored per call, so a sync that yields under 10-13 wei of proceeds pays none. Not exploitable at 18 decimals: each such sync costs far more gas than it saves.

## Round 26 (2026-10-02, owner decision: fix round 23's read-only reentrancy window on the redemption rate)

Round 23 found that `StocksStaking.redeem()` burned the TST before paying out the stock, so that between its transfers `quoteRedeem` / `redeemableStock` published a rate higher than any rate ever settled, and left it documented rather than fixed because the mitigation was the owner's call. The owner has now made it: fixed.

**The change (three lines reordered in `StocksStaking.redeem()`):** the redeemer's stock and the protocol's cut are transferred first, and the TST burn is last. The two sides of the published ratio still move one transfer at a time, and the views are still not covered by `nonReentrant`, so an intermediate state is still observable by a stock token with a transfer callback. What changed is its direction: the numerator now leads the denominator, so every intermediate state reads at or BELOW the rate from before the redemption, which is itself at or below the settled rate. A consumer that trusts the quote mid-call is now conservative rather than overpaying. Nothing is paid for free by the reordering: the burn is in the same transaction and reverts all three transfers if it fails.

**Tests (`test/StocksAuditR23.solodit.t.sol`, rewritten from proving the defect to pinning the fix; 6 tests, was 4):** the observer now records the highest and lowest rate across EVERY callback rather than one of them. With the callback before balances move, the highest rate seen during a 10%-of-supply redemption equals the pre-redemption rate (90.0e18) and never exceeds it; with the callback after balances move (the ERC777 shape) every observation is strictly below it. A fuzz over the redeemed share of supply and the callback placement holds the same bound for every size. A redemption without allowance reverts and pays nothing, with the treasury untouched. The plain-token control is unchanged.

**Negative control, recorded:** with the old burn-first order restored, 3 of the 6 fail: 99.999e18 seen against 90.0e18 with the pre-transfer callback, 90.999e18 against 90.0e18 with the post-transfer one, and the fuzz found a redemption size that read **298.7e18 against 90.0e18**, more than three times the real rate. Round 23 had measured only the 10%-of-supply case (1,013 bps); the overstatement grows without bound as the redeemed share approaches the whole supply, which makes the original finding somewhat worse than it was recorded and the fix correspondingly more worthwhile.

**Not changed, and why:** `claim()` has an analogous instant. It zeroes the claimer's reward in storage and then transfers it, so a pre-transfer callback on the stock token would see `redeemableStock()` too high by exactly the reward in flight. That ordering is checks-effects-interactions on a function that pays out, and reversing it would be the less safe trade; the amount is one staker's claim rather than a share of supply; and it is unreachable for the same reason as before (no attested token has transfer callbacks). Recorded so the scope of this fix is not overstated: it closes the redemption path, which was the one that scaled.

**Status of round 23's finding 1:** fixed here. Finding 2 (no deadline on buy/sell/redeem) remains informational and unchanged.

## In-kind redemption (new in V12)

`StocksStaking.redeem(tstAmount, minStockOut)` lets any TST holder burn TST and receive a pro rata share of the treasury's stock, in kind (the wrapped share itself). It is a feature of the treasury contract; there is no admin, switch or parameter.

**Rate.** `stockOut = redeemableStock * tstAmount / nonBurnedSupply`, less the pool's own cost. `redeemableStock` is the treasury's stock balance minus what stakers have already earned (the same expression a governance liquidation protects), so earned rewards are never touched. `nonBurnedSupply` is the total supply less the burn address and counts every other token, including the pool's, so trading in the pool cannot move the rate. The cost is read from the pool's own launch record (`launches(poolId).feeBps`, 10%): 2% goes to the protocol and 8% stays in the treasury, which raises the value per remaining token. The gross rounds down and the cost rounds up, so rounding always favors the treasury. `quoteRedeem` returns the exact payout.

**Accounting.** Stock nobody is owed leaves first (stock received while rewards were paused, then stream that ran while nobody was staked); only the remainder shortens stakers' future stream (`_shrinkRewardsBy` lowers `lastNotifiedBalance` and `rewardRate`). Staked TST must be unstaked first. Redemption is never blocked by a liquidation: blocking it would let governance freeze exits.

**What was checked.**
- `test/StocksStaking.redemption.t.sol` (35 tests): the worked example, quote equals payout, earned rewards untouched even when redeeming everything, the stream shrinking by exactly what left, every revert, the pool's own cost setting, live and expired liquidation orders, pause, no stakers, donation then redeem, burning to move the rate, a sandwich around a redemption, stake/unstake/claim around a redemption, reentrancy from the stock token, and fuzzed and random-walk accounting.
- `test/StocksRedemption.adversarial.t.sol` (8 tests, including gas): flash-borrowed TST cannot be redeemed and repaid at a profit, arbitrage below the rate is profitable and harms nobody, buying and redeeming at or above the rate always loses, splitting matches an independent integer reference and stays under the ceiling below, dust spam leaks no value, traders cannot move the rate, and the quote depends only on the treasury and the supply.
- `test/StocksRedemption.scenarios.t.sol` (6 tests, real hook, real curve, staking and governor): redemption while stakers are earning, while a liquidation proposal is pending (the proposal still executes after its voter redeems everything, because a redemption can never empty the pot: the pool's and stakers' TST stay outstanding; a redemption during the voting window does not affect the snapshotted vote), and while a TWAMM liquidation order is live, expired but unsettled, and settled. In each, earned rewards stay claimable in full, the hook stays fully backed, TST is conserved, settlement never lowers the rate, and a second liquidation still works.
- Whole-system invariants (`test/StocksSystem.invariant.t.sol`) and the staking invariants (`test/invariant/StocksStakingHandler.sol`) now include redemption in their random walk. Heavy runs on two seeds: staking invariants 720,000 calls, system invariants 800,000 calls, a 60,000-run redemption walk, all passing.
- `test/StocksLifecycle.realInk.t.sol` redeems against the real Uniswap v4 PoolManager and the real AAPLx wrapper.
- Slither over the changed contract: three new items, all intentional and reviewed.
- Mutation testing: 20 deliberately planted bugs in the redemption path. All are now caught. One (a removed reentrancy guard) initially survived because the reentrancy test was failing for an unrelated reason; the test was fixed and now fails without the guard.

**Design properties (reviewed, not bugs).**
- Splitting a redemption into several smaller ones lets the redeemer win back part of the cost they paid, because each piece raises the value per remaining token. With infinitely many pieces, a holder of fraction f of the supply receives at most (1 - (1 - f)^0.92) / (0.92 * f) times what one redemption would pay: at most about 8.7% for a holder of the entire supply, about 0.4% for a 10% holder, and it levels off after five to ten pieces. It costs the redeemer gas, takes nothing from other holders, and is tested.
- While a liquidation is running, the TST it will buy and burn still counts as outstanding until it is claimed, so the rate is slightly understated in that window. Anyone can claim the burn first.
- The redemption rate is a share of what the treasury holds. It is not a price guarantee, and how large the treasury is relative to the token's market value depends on trading volume.
- Only pools launched from a V12 (or V11) factory have `redeemableStock` and the launch record it reads. Earlier generations cannot get redemption later.
- Redemption pays out wrapped shares, so it inherits the wrapper trust assumptions in "Dividends" below.

## Dividends (a design choice, not a code path)

xStocks pay dividends by raising a rebasing multiplier on the raw token. The treasury holds the stock through xStocks' non-rebasing ERC-4626 wrapper, whose share count never changes and whose `convertToAssets` rate follows the live multiplier. So a dividend makes each treasury share redeemable for more raw stock with no transaction and nothing to claim. `StocksStaking` deliberately does nothing about it, and `_notifyReward()` only counts real balance increases, so a rising rate is never mistaken for new reward income. The dividend value is captured when governance liquidates the treasury. Stakers and post-graduation sellers receive wrapped shares, which likewise convert into more raw stock over time.

This rests on one external assumption, checked by `test/RealXStockWrapper.dividendrate.t.sol` on a live Ink fork for AAPLx, MSFTx, KOx and PEPx: the wrapper's rate equals the raw token's live `multiplier()`, the wrapper reads `getCurrentMultiplier()` on every conversion (a mocked 5% rebase moves the real wrapper's rate immediately), and `totalAssets` equals wrapped supply times the multiplier.

## Documented design properties (reviewed, not flagged as bugs)

- `StocksCurveFactory`, `StocksStakingFactory` and `StocksGovernorFactory` are permissionless spawners. Anyone can call `deploy()` directly, but the spawned contract is bound to `msg.sender` at construction (for example `StocksStakingFactory` passes `msg.sender` as the staking contract's `curve`, the only address allowed to call `setPool`), so a spawn made outside `StocksLaunchFactory` is not connected to any real launch.
- `StocksHook`'s reward ping (`notifyRewardAmount()`) is intentionally unchecked, and `StocksStaking.notifyRewardAmount()` is intentionally unguarded: it only does idempotent balance accounting, and a missed ping is picked up by the next call.
- Exact-output swaps are disallowed (`ExactOutputNotSupported`), because the flywheel routing math assumes an exact-input swap.
- `StocksGraduator.graduate()` pulls seed funds with `safeTransferFrom` (pull, not push-then-call), a deliberate fix for an earlier reentrancy hijack.
- `TSTToken` never reduces `totalSupply()`: a "burn" is a transfer to `0x...dEaD`, so governance quorum subtracts the burn-address balance.
- Hook ownership is renounced in the `StocksHook` constructor, and there is no kill switch.
- Sybil splitting across wallets defeats any per-address anti-snipe cap. This is inherent and accepted; the cap only stops a single address from exceeding its own limit.
